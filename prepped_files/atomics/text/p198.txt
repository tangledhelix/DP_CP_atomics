The pressure thus built up inside the shell practically instantaneously pushes
outward in all directions with enormous force. It bursts the container, which
flies outward, and pushes the air at great speed. This causes the terrific blast
effect of modern explosives.

Now one might imagine that TNT and nitroglycerin, being such violent
explosives, would have much more energy in them than such common fuels as
gasoline and coal. Actually, TNT produces one-third as much heat as gasoline,
pound for pound, and only about one-half as much as coal. Nitroglycerin has
even less heat energy than TNT (about half as much). The main difference
in explosive power between the TNT and coal is in the rapidity with which
the chemical change takes place. A pound of coal, burning over a long period
of time, produces more heat. But the gases and the heat are spread out over
such a long time that there is no high pressure or explosive effect. Of course
the amount of energy produced is a factor in the explosion.

Thus, the two significant features in any bomb must be the liberation of a
large amount of energy and the very rapid rate at which this energy is produced.
But while the high explosives mentioned above derive their energy
from ordinary chemical reactions, atomic bombs involve the transmutation of
elements and the liberation of enormous energies derived from the annihilation
of matter.

Our discussion of the chain-reacting piles using ordinary uranium and
graphite has indicated the source of the energy that is liberated. In a chain-reacting
pile, the multiplication factor is maintained at exactly 1. In other
words, 100 uranium atoms that are undergoing fission give rise to just enough
neutrons to cause fissions in exactly 100 other uranium atoms. When this
growth factor is allowed to be more than 1, then the chain reaction builds up
very rapidly until enormous numbers of atoms are undergoing fission and
liberating energy.

However, even after the uranium pile was successfully operated as early as
1942, the possibility of creating an atomic bomb was still in doufrt. One important
question hinged on the rapidity with which the process could take
place. If the release of energy was too slow, or if the bomb flew apart before a
sufficient portion of the energy had been released, then it would hardly have
been worth all the time and money spent on it (from the point of view of the
military considerations of the moment).
