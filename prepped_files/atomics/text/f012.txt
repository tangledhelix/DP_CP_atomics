achievement is the work of the minds and the labors of many men, and how
one phase of science, the development of atomic energy, has been built brick
by brick into a vast cathedral. Or is it a vast monster which may destroy us?
During the coming years, governmental bodies the world over will take
actions which may tend to regulate science. Regulation imposed by governments
upon scientific endeavors, in my opinion, is undesirable, for science
itself is not responsible for the bad or the good effects that result from increasing
knowledge. Science supplies us with knowledge, but the question
of how we use it lies entirely apart from science. Some even propose that we
destroy all scientific work, but this would compel us to accept a very low
standard of living and probably a decrease in the total population of the

earth. I think it unlikely that we would choose this alternative. The only
hopeful approach to the problem is to discipline ourselves in regard to the
use of our powerful knowledge. In democratic countries this cannot be done
except through the will of the people themselves; we cannot exercise our will
intelligently unless we have a clear understanding of the problems involved.
This book should contribute a wider comprehension of the scientific method
of acquiring knowledge.

Harold C. Urey
