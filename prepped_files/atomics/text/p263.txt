In order to test this idea, Dr. Kruger started with pieces of cancerous tissue
taken from diseased animals. Such tissue will continue to grow when it is
implanted in a mouse. In other words, the test mouse will develop cancer.
He then soaked some cancerous tissues in boric acid solution in order to allow
them to absorb boron atoms. After this treatment they were irradiated with
slow neutrons. Now the irradiated tissue was implanted in healthy mice.
If the mice remained healthy it would mean that the active components of the
cancerous tissue soaked in boric acid had been killed by the slow neutron
treatment. Enough destruction of the cancerous tissue was observed to indicate
that the method had possibilities. Work along these lines was interrupted
by the war but has now been vigorously resumed.

Of course, the basic initial problem is to find methods of selectively depositing
boron, lithium, or other suitable chemicals in the cancerous tissue. It is
known that certain dyes have the ability to pass through and localize in
damaged tissue. We might attempt to make lithium salts of these dyes or
to find other compounds containing the desired element that will be preferentially
absorbed by the cancerous tissue.

The powerful tools of the chemist should now be harnessed along with
those of the nuclear physicist and the physician. Thousands of test-tube
experiments using cancerous tissue and healthy tissue will have to be tested
for selective absorption of hundreds of possible chemicals. Fortunately, the
Edisonian approach is not strange to the practicing chemist. Paul Ehrlich's
pioneer arsenical for syphilis was named "606" because that many compounds
were tested before one produced results. Edison would frequently
remark that he had found at least several hundred ways how not to accomplish
a desired result. The climaxing discovery by Midgely and his coworkers
of tetraethyl lead as an antiknock agent for gasoline was made after thousands
of compounds had been tried. To one who has seen the successful developments
of the atomic-bomb project stem from "impossible" beginnings, there
appears little doubt that the application of a similar effort here will produce
remarkable results. The field is wide open for many new discoveries in this
vital experimentation.
