200,000 lives in the United States are claimed by cancer each year, accounting
for about 10 per cent of all deaths. Its cause and very nature are still dark mysteries.
We know that a healthy organ is a functioning composite of countless
numbers of microscopic cells, all performing specialized operations and engaging
in varied chemical processes. In cancerous tissue, there appears to be
an anarchic and uncontrolled growth of cells causing a serious interference
with the normal healthy function of the organ in question. As long as this
growth remains in one spot and does relatively little harm, it is termed a benign
growth, or tumor. When, however, portions of this diseased tissue break
away from their original location, enter the blood stream, and lodge in other
organs, we have a malignant cancer. If the growth arises from various covering
layers of the body tissue, such as the stomach, the cancer is referred to as a
carcinoma. Cancers originating or lodging in connective or supporting tissue,
such as bone, are called sarcomas. Many external cancers, such as those involving
the skin, lips, mouth, and occasionally the throat, have been discovered at
an early stage and cured. Internal cancers, such as those of the lung, stomach,
and intestines, are usually detected at such an advanced stage that only a small
fraction have yielded to treatment. There remains the hope that some readily
applied test will be discovered that will give us suitable warning at an early
stage so as to make early diagnosis and treatment possible.

Thus far, the ordinary techniques of medicine have been of little avail.
However, radium and x-rays combined with surgery have shown signs of
promising results. Wide vistas have now been opened with new tools furnished
by the same brilliant scientists who have helped to usher in the Atomic
Age.

Accidental discovery of the effects of radium on tissue . . .

The first observation of the effects of radiation on living tissue was made
accidentally by Becquerel. The world was already indebted to him for having
discovered the remarkable property of natural radioactivity in uranium. He
noticed a severe external wound on his abdomen shortly after he had carried
around a tiny vial of radium salts in his vest pocket. He immediately surmised
that this wound, resembling a deep burn, was caused by the radiation coming
from the radium salts. Apparently these radiations could destroy healthy
tissue. Could they also destroy cancerous tissue?
