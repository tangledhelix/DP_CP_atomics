Joining in the hunt at this stage was the team comprising Dr.
Ernest O. Lawrence, Glenn T. Seaborg, Emilio Ségrè, Joseph W. Kennedy,
and Arthur C. Wahl. In December, 1940, after bombarding uranium with
deuterons, they obtained an isotope of element 94, plutonium. The amounts
available were still so small that radioactive tracer methods had to be used.
Nevertheless, many chemical properties of this element could be studied.
In order to produce larger quantities of plutonium, several hundred pounds
of a uranium salt, uranyl nitrate, were bombarded with neutrons for long
periods in 1941. Small quantities of plutonium having an atomic weight
of 239 were identified in the products, isolated, and studied intensively.

Thus plutonium could be manufactured
from uranium 238, the most numerous isotope
uranium, merely by bombarding it with
neutrons. The unstable U239 formed is an
artificially radioactive substance and changes
itself into neptunium (element 93) and
then into plutonium (element 94)--emitting
two electrons in succession in the process. The
uranium 239 that is first formed decays rapidly,
with a half-life of about 23 minutes, to
form neptunium. The latter is also unstable
and decays with a half-life of about 2.3 days
to form plutonium.

It was also discovered that the plutonium,
which is fairly stable, can be used like U235
to obtain large amounts of atomic energy.
When bombarded with neutrons, the plutonium
atom splits or "fisses" and produces
more neutrons. These neutrons, in turn, can
then split other plutonium atoms and keep a
chain reaction going.
