The spectroscope, an instrument designed for this purpose of analyzing
spectra, is today one of the most valuable tools of many sciences. To the chemist
it provides an extremely sensitive method of detecting the presence of minute
quantities of all elements. Spectacular use of this instrument has been made
in criminology for tracing the origin of materials, such as scrapings from
shoes and fingernails. To the astronomer this instrument is as important as
a ruler to a mechanic. With it he analyzes the chemical constituents in the sun
and stars, classifies star types, estimates star brightness and temperature, and
deduces distances. He can even tell whether a star is approaching us or receding
from us, and he can measure the speed with which this is taking
place.

This spectroscope has been invaluable to the atomic scientist in a number
of ways. It has enabled him to detect the presence of minute amounts of various
substances in his search for the secrets of atomic changes. And it has also
provided important clues upon which modern theories of atoms are based.
In a subsequent chapter we shall see how the spectrum of hydrogen provided
a basis upon which Niels Bohr created a useful model of the motions that
take place within atoms. It also provided basic information leading to the
discovery of "heavy water."

In 1800 Sir William Herschel, experimenting with the heating effects produced
by light, tried placing a thermometer in the dark region of the spectrum
near the red light. He found that the temperature rose. This pointed to the
existence of waves near the red end of the spectrum which were invisible to
the eye. These waves are known to us as infrared, or heat rays. When photographs
were made of the spectrum it was found that there was a considerable
amount of invisible light beyond the violet. Today we refer to these waves as
ultraviolet.
