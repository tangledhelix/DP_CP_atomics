Several million years using early magnet . . .

With the vapor of a uranium compound used as the source of ions, this
method of electromagnetic separation would enable us to obtain absolutely
pure U235 immediately. But the question was, How much could we produce?
It turned out that only insignificant amounts of absolutely pure U235 could
be obtained with apparatus existing during the early stages of the bomb
project.

The difficulties were numerous, as was the case with all problems on the
project. To obtain the ions, it is necessary to introduce uranium in the form
of a gas of one of its compounds into a chamber. We then bombard these
atoms with electrons or some other form of radiation so as to knock out electrons
from the outer rings and produce ions. Some of these ions are made to
pass through a slit where the attraction of a near-by plate (oppositely charged)
speeds them up. It is important at this point for all ions to have practically
the same speed. A small fraction of these ions pass through another hole in
the attracting plate, and the beam is thus narrowed down sharply. Note that
many ions which strike the first plate without passing through the hole are
eliminated. In addition many ions which get through the first hole strike the
second plate and are stopped. Only a small fraction of the original ions go
through the second hole to form a beam. As a result the yield is extremely
small.

Nevertheless, this method of separating U235 from uranium offered the
only quick method of getting pure U235. In 1941, A. O. Nier's mass spectrometer
was put to work and succeeded in collecting U235 at the rate of about
2 billionths of an ounce per hour. At this rate it would take about 2 million
years to make a 2-pound piece of U235!

Improving the yield . . .

For purposes of mass production, it became necessary to use much larger
magnets than those generally used for mass spectrometers. Consequently
work was begun in 1941 with the magnet of Lawrence's cyclotron at Berkeley,
Calif. This giant magnet had poles that were more than 3 feet in diameter.
Its powerful magnetic field speeded up the separation process considerably
and led to important discoveries. This device for separating U235 from uranium
came to be known as the calutron (contraction of California University
Cyclotron).
