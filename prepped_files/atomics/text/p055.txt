Joachimsthal mine in Bohemia. The most important natural mineral found
there, pitchblende, contained an oxide of uranium together with other impurities.
To their surprise, this impure natural source of uranium was several
times more radioactive than the
pure compounds prepared from the
ore.

Madame Curie at once drew a
brilliant yet simple inference. She
had been convinced from the start
that the spontaneous emission of
energy from uranium was a property
connected entirely with the
uranium atom. Hence, since all
compounds of uranium contain
these atoms, their emission should
be equal regardless of their source.
But pitchblende was an exception.
It thus became clear that there must
be present some other material,
hidden away in the pitchblende,
responsible for the unusual emission
of radiation. Further, this material
must be present in extremely minute amounts, since she could account
by simple chemical analysis for well over 99 per cent of the materials present
in the ore. This observation was later strikingly confirmed--in the discovery
and isolation of radium and the preparation of sources of radioactivity millions
of times as powerful as uranium.

Finding the needle in the haystack . . .

Searching for a needle in a haystack might be considered a relatively simple
task compared to the problem facing the Curies. At the start, they knew very
little about the material being sought. Today we know that the radium they
were seeking is present in such small amounts that almost 100 tons of ore
must be processed to obtain only 1 ounce of radium. Many impurities were
present to complicate the separation. These included silicon, lead, calcium,
aluminum, barium, and iron.

Finally, after many laborious operations, some crystals were prepared containing
radium and barium, in the form of their chlorides. However, these
crystals still contained less than 1/1,000 of 1 per cent of radium, corresponding
to one small drop in a gallon of water.
