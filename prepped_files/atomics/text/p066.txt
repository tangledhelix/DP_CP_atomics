
Chapter Seven

Discovering Atomic Architecture

Leading to the key problem . . .

During the first decade of the present century, the intensive study of
various rays in the cathode-ray tube and of the radiations from
radium had revealed a number of important conclusions about
atoms and the particles of which they are composed.

There was no doubt that the atom existed. All the work of the chemist and
much of the labor of the physicist had proved that conclusively. There was
no doubt, also, that the atom contained small fundamental particles having
mass and electric charge. The electron, the unit of negative electricity, and the
proton, the unit of positive electricity, were already well known. The key
question was, How are these particles arranged in the atom? Is the mass of
the atom distributed uniformly, as if it resembled a baseball? Or, taking the
other extreme, does the atom resemble our own solar system, in which the sun
may be considered the nucleus or core around which move small planets separated
by great distances from the sun and from each other? Within 16 years,
this problem was solved. And the method of solution might be termed atomic
artillery practice.
