Occasionally it is found that a particle having lower energy than is normally
required to enter the nucleus manages to get inside the nuclear home near the
bottom of the depression. Of course, this would be impossible according to
the "common sense" inherent in the familiar laws of moving bodies. But it
turns out that there is a certain probability of this remarkable effect taking
place when particles of atomic size arc concerned. We could modify our
roller-coaster model by boring a tunnel somewhere through the hill as indicated
by C in the drawing. In that case an occasional particle that does not
have enough energy to "climb the wall of repulsion" might happen to enter
this "tunnel" and get into the nucleus. This has been called the tunnel effect
and plays an important role in atomic physics.

Neutrons do not have to "climb a hill" . . .

In the case of a neutron, the high wall does not exist because there are no
repulsive forces to keep it out of the
nucleus. We could thus consider the
track as flat, with the neutron able
to enter any nucleus which happens
to be in the way. For this reason
practically all neutrons wind up by
being captured by some nucleus. In
that enevent the neutron "tenant"
changes the structure of the nucleus
and occasionally makes it unstable
so that it tends to break up into
more stable forms. Thus, gamma
rays or assorted particles may be shot out of the nucleus as the combination
gropes for a stable arrangement.

of four fundamental building blocks of the atom--the electron, proton, neutron,
and positron. Our atom guns and manifold instruments for detecting
modern alchemy are now lined up to play their part in the most dramatic
part of our story--the achievement of large-scale atomic energy, the conversion
of matter into energy on a practical basis.
