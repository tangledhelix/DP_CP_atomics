Neutrons are also formed during fission . . .

Now another vital fact was uncovered. It was shown that when a single
neutron bombarded an atom of U235, other neutrons shot out of the atom,
along with the fission fragments. Von Halban, Joliot, and Kowarski calculated
that, on the average, 3.5 neutrons were emitted per fission. Anderson,
Fermi, and Hanstein, working in the United States, later reported
about 2 neutrons per fission. Many other investigators tackled this problem.
In a careful study, Zinn and Szilard, writing in Physical Review, estimated
that 2.3 secondary neutrons, on the average, were emitted as the result of
the splitting of a U235 atom.

This fact opened up new possibilities. If one neutron could cause a
uranium 235 atom to split and release a large amount of energy, and at the
same time produce a number of new neutrons, then it might be possible for
these secondary neutrons to repeat the process and cause additional U235
atoms to split. Thus, for the first time the possibility of a chain reaction
was established, in which one neutron would start a whole series of fissions
which would not stop until all atoms of U235 had been split.

Large-scale atomic energy might now be possible . . .

Of hundreds of well-known nuclear reactions, uranium fission offered
the first opportunity to obtain nuclear energy on a practical scale. As far
back as 1932, Cockroft and Walton, working in England, bombarded a
target of lithium metal with 700,000-volt protons. Two alpha particles were
produced as a result of each successful collision. The combined kinetic
energy of the alpha particles was 17 M.e.v. Simple calculations proved that
this large energy came from the mass that was lost in this reaction. Here
then was an example of the conversion of mass into energy. Why not use
this reaction or similar ones to obtain atomic power i
