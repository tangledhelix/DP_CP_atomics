Putting the instruments to work . . .

Here was a case where the tools of the physicist that had been so useful
in earlier work resulted in an interplay of vital information. Electroscopes were
used in order to measure the total intensity of the radiation coming from above.
This was accomplished by placing lead and other metallic radiation shields
on the bottom of these instruments to block radiations coming from the earth.

The cloud chamber was found especially helpful in this detective work.
Ordinarily, photons do not leave any tracks in cloud-chamber photographs.
But an occasional electron knocked out of an atom by a photon had sufficient
velocity to leave a wake of water droplets in its path which could be photographed.
The general appearance of the track photographed in this instrument
was a clue as to the nature of the charged particle that had passed through.
Tracks made by an electron are beaded and light in intensity. Proton and alpha-particle
tracks are heavy. We have already made use of the fact that a moving
charged particle is deflected by a magnetic field, so that the original straight
path becomes an arc of a circle. By placing a magnetic field at right angles
to the direction of incoming cosmic rays, these curvatures could be photographed
and their energies estimated. The fact that a positive particle will
swerve oppositely from a negative particle enables us to discover the kind of
charge carried.

An important instrument used in these studies was the Geiger counter
tube. In its simplest form it consists of a tube into which have been sealed
two metallic terminals. These terminals are attached to a high-voltage source
and filled with a suitable gas. Ordinarily no electric current will flow across
the tube. But when a high-energy particle enters the tube, ionization of the
gas occurs and a pulse of current flows. This current is then amplified and can
operate a mechanical counting device. In this manner, this simple instrument
is able to detect the presence of individual atomic projectiles. When several
of these instruments were placed in a line and arranged to record a particle
only when all instruments went off together, it was possible to determine the
number of ionizing particles that descended on the earth from a very definite
direction. With the use of an amplifier it was possible to note the entrance
