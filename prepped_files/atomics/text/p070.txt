With this theory Bohr was easily able to explain the frequencies of certain
groups of lines in the hydrogen spectrum. The agreement between theory and
observation was so remarkably close that his picture of the atom received immediate
and widespread attention. But at the same time the concept of a
solar-system type of atom was even more firmly entrenched, and Einstein's
photon light package received greater support.

The Rutherford-Bohr atom model has been supplanted today by other pictures
which are more complex. But all these pictures contain the following
essential elements : a small concentrated nucleus, electrons rotating about the
nucleus, empty space throughout most of the atom, energy levels for electrons,
and photons shooting out whenever the electron moves from a higher to a
lower level. This will suffice for the purpose of atom smashing, in which our
main concern is with the nucleus rather than with the electrons. New evidence
about the structure of this nucleus came very soon after the solar-system at
was brought forth.

Giving each element a number . . .

The evidence was now very strong in favor of the solar-system atom. The
positive charge in the atom was located in a very small core, the nucleus.
Now the question arose: What is this charge for each element? How many
positive charges are on the carbon nucleus? On the oxygen nucleus? Can two
different elements, such as gold and silver, have the same nuclear charge?
Or is it the nuclear charge that distinguishes one element from another?

We have seen how Rutherford uncovered many clues while bombarding
atoms with alpha particles. He expected that the positively charged alpha particles
would be repelled by the positive charge on the nucleus. By measuring
these repulsive effects, Rutherford was able to calculate the approximate
positive charge on the nucleus for a number of elements. This was an important
step forward.

But a far more important and precise answer to the question of the amount
of positive charge on the nucleus was given by a young British investigator,
Moseley, a short while before he was killed in battle in the First World War.
