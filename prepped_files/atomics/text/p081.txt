
Very high voltages by gradually building up electric charge . . .

How do these machines operate? In order to obtain very high speed particles,
it is necessary to do two things: First, obtain a source of high voltage. Then,
place charged particles in this region, and they will speed up to the charged
plate that is attracting them. How can we obtain this high voltage? An
ordinary dry cell furnishes about 1.5 volts. A typical automobile storage battery
will furnish 6 volts. By hooking together 1,000 of the latter, we could
obtain 6,000 volts, but that would not be very convenient. Direct-current house
voltage furnishes about no volts. If we could use the third rail of a typical
electric railway system, we would have about 500 volts. Special transformers can
supply only several hundred thousand volts. To achieve the millions of volts
needed in atom smashing requires much skill and ingenuity.

One of the methods for developing extremely high voltages was suggested
and developed by R. J. Van de Graaff, a young electrical engineer and Rhodes
scholar, early in the thirties. His instrument is called the Van de Graaff, or
electrostatic, generator.

In an early model, capable of hurling multimillion-volt "lightning" bolts,
two giant 15-foot spheres, mounted on insulated supports 6 feet wide and 20
feet high, were made to accumulate enormous amounts of electric charge.
The charge was supplied at the base of the insulating support by a 10,000-volt
source. A silk belt picked up this charge and carried it up to the inside of the
sphere, where the mutual repulsion of the charges caused them to scatter to the
outside. One of the spheres was charged up positively and the other negatively.
The charge was gradually accumulated until an electrical tension of several
million volts was built up.
