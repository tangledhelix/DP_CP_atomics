
The methods of detection of the large amounts of energy for each atom of
uranium split by a neutron were rather simple with the techniques at hand.
One method consisted of connecting an "ionization chamber" similar to a
Geiger counter to an oscilloscope ("scope") screen. The passage of a high-speed
particle ionized the molecules of air in the chamber and produced a
sudden surge of current, which showed up on the screen as a zigzag vertical
line or "pip." The greater the energy of the particle, the greater was the ionization
produced and the resulting height of the.zigzag pip on the screen.
The height of the pip was therefore a measure of the energy of the particle.
When a piece of uranium was introduced into the chamber, the ordinary small
pips due to its natural radioactivity were observed. However, when a source
of neutrons (such as radium bombarding beryllium) was brought near the
uranium, large pips, equivalent in energy to ioo M.e.v. or more, were observed.
The cloud chamber could also be used to show these high-energy
fission products.

These experiments arc so simple that one might be amazed that the high-energy
particles resulting from fission had not been discovered much sooner.
But, as with all discoveries in science, it is one thing to repeat a simple experiment
once the path has been blazed, but quite another to select the original
proper procedures and interpret the results correctly.

Collecting the fission products . . .

Another ingenious procedure shed additional light on the nature of the
fission reaction. The fragments resulting from the uranium breakdown should
have sufficient energy to tear themselves completely away from the uranium
sample. In that case, it should easily be possible to catch them on a collector
placed near but not necessarily touching the uranium sample. Various collecting
surfaces were used, including cylinders of bakelite, a water surface, a
pad of cigarette papers, and a glass plate. In all cases, the collector surfaces
became radioactive. Furthermore, the radioactive elements caught by the collector
were identical with those produced in the bulk of the uranium sample.
The collecting surface could be placed at a reasonable distance away from
the uranium, because the high-energy fission fragments could travel about
i inch in air before they lost all their energy by collision and were stopped.
The unusually large distances traveled by these fission fragments was another
indication of their high speed and large energy.
