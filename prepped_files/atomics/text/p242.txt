
There is another possible method of propelling the space ship by using
atomic-storage-battery material to eject particles that will leave the ship.
Thus, if we had several pounds of some radioactive material that decayed
rather rapidly with a half-life of hours or days, the particles ejected from the
atoms with speeds of tens of thousands of miles per second would provide
a tremendous propelling force.

Let us assume that we have 100 pounds of a radioactive material of relatively
short half-life that breaks down to liberate 2 pounds of alpha particles having
velocities of 20,000 miles per second. If this material could be spread out in
some way on the back of the space ship, the ejection of large numbers of high-speed
particles would provide the kick needed to drive the ship forward
Although emitted in various directions, the particles would provide sufficient
momentum to a small 1,000-pound ship to give it a speed of about 10 miles
per second--sufficient to free itself from the earth's attraction. As pointed out
by G. Gamow (who has made the above suggestion) a major obstacle here
would be the necessity for spreading the material out very thin, since the
alphas cannot even penetrate a sheet of paper. If it were spread thickly, only
the outer surface would be effective in propulsion.

From an energy viewpoint, the fission of U235 would be far more efficient
for this purpose. The U235 splits into almost equal heavy fragments, each
with velocities of about 100,000 miles per second. In this case 100 pounds of
U235 would yield close to 100 pounds of fission fragments. At least half of
these, traveling in a forward direction, must be stopped by some shielding.
Rough estimates show that if we had a 10,000-pound rocket, the expulsion of
50 pounds of material at speeds of 100,000 miles per second or so would provide
enough momentum for several dozen trips away from the earth.

A major problem here would be to get the U235 atoms to "fiss" at will.
Spreading the material in thin layers at the back of the ship would be necessary
to get sufficient propulsion and to avoid the waste of fission products
burying themselves in U235. But this automatically destroys the possibility
of getting sufficient size to start a chain reaction. The fission of the U235
atoms would then have to be accomplished by using an outside neutron source.
The usual radium-beryllium neutron source is far too feeble for such a purpose.
Building a pile containing U235 or plutonium as a neutron source would
be a possibility. A visionary possibility would be to design a lightweight high-intensity
atom-gun machine to bombard sheets of metal backing the U235
to produce neutrons. Such an arrangement could be easily controlled, and
any desired amount of neutrons and subsequent fissions could be maintained
or stopped.
