Pinning down an atomic building block--the electron . . .

What were these mysterious particles coming from the negative terminal
of the tube? The outstanding English investigator J. J. Thomson, just a few
years before 1900, obtained some important clues. He made the particles
swerve by placing charged electric plates near them and measuring the amounts
of deflection. He also made them swerve by placing magnets near by. After
these and other measurements, he arrived at several important conclusions.
He found that the particles traveled with speeds of tens of thousands of miles
per second.

How much electric charge did they carry? How much did they weigh? He
could not obtain the answer to either of these questions. But he was able to
show that the charge of a particle divided by its weight was always the same
regardless of the kind of gas used in the tube or type of metal used for the
electrodes. Thus it would seem that this particle is a fundamental building
block found in all matter. Today we call these cathode-ray particles electrons.
These electron beams in modern versions of Thomson's tube are today
serving in such important devices as the television tube, the oscilloscope used
in radar, and the electron microscope. The all-important modern field of
electronics grew out of these pioneering experiments to unearth the nature of
the electron.

The electron crops up in many places . . .

Soon this new actor was found taking a number of roles. If you look at the
space around the glowing wire of an electric light bulb, nothing unusual is
visible to your unaided eye. However, a positive plate sealed into the lamp
would show that negative particles are actually "evaporating" from the hot wire
and traveling across the space to the plate. In fact, you have here the makings of
a simple radio tube. Edison was the first to notice this effect but was unable
to explain it. Experimenting with the aid of magnets and charged plates, it
soon became clear to scientists that these evaporating particles were electrons.
In this case the high temperature led to a greatly increased to-and-fro motion
among the atoms making up the wire. As a result, it became easier for electrons
to escape from the heated wire.
