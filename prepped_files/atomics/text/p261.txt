
Neutrons now join the attack on cancer . . .

With the discovery of the neutron by Chadwick in 1932, a new high-energy
source could be added to the arsenal of weapons attacking this scourge of
mankind. At first, neutron sources were highly inadequate. Using scarce
supplies of radium, the issuing alpha particles would be made to strike a
boron target, resulting in the liberation of small quantities of neutrons. In
the same year, the ingenuity of Dr. E. O. Lawrence provided the world of
science with a new and remarkable tool for speeding up charged particles, the
cyclotron. Using this instrument, more copious supplies of neutrons became
available. One of the principal ways in which this could be accomplished
was the bombardment of beryllium with high-speed deuterons from a cyclotron.

Using deuterons that have an energy greater than 1 M.e.v., the number of
neutrons that are produced in this collision process are more abundant than
the yield from many other kinds of collisions. When 5-M.e.v. deuterons are
used, the neutrons produced have an energy range up to 9 M.e.v. If lithium
is the target instead of beryllium, neutrons having energies up to 20 M.e.v.
can be produced.

Why may we expect different results when neutron beams are used in
cancer therapy? In order to answer this question, we may look into the
general action of both neutrons and x-rays on matter. As a result of careful
studies, we have come to the conclusion that the primary x-rays and neutron
beams themselves produce little effect on tissue directly. When x-rays plow
through matter, their initial effect is to knock off electrons from atoms and
send them shooting out with high energies. These electrons, in turn, plow
through neighboring atoms and cause secondary ionization. But they arc
relatively poor ionizing agents because of their small mass. This factor, however,
gives them a greater range within the tissue before they are stopped.

Neutron bullets, on the other hand, act differently. Being heavier, they are
able to create high-speed ions from the molecules and atoms of tissue. But
these heavy ions of high energy have very short ranges. They plow through
a short distance in the tissue with much greater destructive effect than is
produced by the fast electrons resulting from x-rays. The neutron bombardment
thus produces a highly localized destruction close to the paths of the
neutrons, whereas x-ray treatment produces more diffuse destruction of tissue
in a general area. For this reason attempts to destroy cancerous tissue by neutron
bombardment have been initiated in a number of laboratories. From what
