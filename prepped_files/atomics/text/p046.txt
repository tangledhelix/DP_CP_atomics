Now we step up the frequency measured by our gadget. Waves from the
x-ray machine in a near-by dentist's office cause a buzzing in our receiver. Faint
static coming from the atmosphere also begins to show up. As we increase the
frequency this static becomes more distinct. We are now entering the region
of very high frequency gamma rays and "soft" cosmic rays. A scientific laboratory
near by causes a great deal of buzzing because it has some radioactive
materials stored away for atom-smashing experiments. We have now reached
the end of the dial and pause to glance over the range through which we
have passed.

In our travels from the radio waves at one end through the infrared, visible
light, ultraviolet, x-rays, and gamma rays, we have traversed a tremendous
range. At the radio end of the dial we have been picking up waves about 1/4
mile in length. At the other end of the dial we have been registering gamma
rays whose length must be measured in fractions of a billionth of an inch.

A rapid glance at the dial shows that the visible portion of the spectrum is
very small. Of the 60 numbers on our dial, only one represents the region
within which our eyes function to give us the sense of sight.

Light also comes in packages . . .

As is the fate of all scientific theories to date, a time comes when new facts
raise questions that inevitably lead to changes in our ideas. In this case the
wave theory crashed head-on into the ghost of Newton's corpuscular theory,
dressed in new garb.

The German Max Planck had been studying the manner in which glowing
bodies give off infrared waves. Others before him had tried unsuccessfully
to account properly for the amounts of infrared rays of different frequency
that were observed. In 1901 Planck found that he could account for the
served facts provided he made a bold assumption about light waves. The
energy of the waves could be emitted by a radiating body only in definite
"packages of energy."
