Using this relatively simple technique, Fermi soon concluded that not one of
the neighbors of uranium, having atomic numbers from 86 to 92, was formed.
Upon disclosure of Fermi's work, other scientists took up the same problem.
Numerous tests were made to identify the radioactive products. By the end
of 1935, more suspects had been eliminated. It appeared pretty clear that all
the elements from atomic number 80 and up, including thallium (81), lead
(82), bismuth (83), and radium (88), could be eliminated.

On the verge of discovery . . .

During 1936 and 1937, the able German scientists Hahn, Meitner, and
Strassman took up the search. Convinced that the active products were elements
beyond uranium (transuranic) they attempted to set up a scheme
that would satisfy their experimental findings. In order to do so, they were
forced to assume the existence of elements all the way from 93 to 97, and
possibly more. Their solution seemed enormously complicated and brought
up disturbing questions, but a more satisfactory solution was lacking.

In 1938, Curie and Savitch, working in France, uncovered some new evidence.
As a matter of fact, they were on the verge of the correct answer but
somehow missed it! On irradiating uranium with neutrons, they found that
one of the radioactive products had a half-life of 3-1/2 hours. They used a
variety of well-known elements as carrier or tracer material in order to track
down the identity of the 3-1/2-hour product. When element number 57, lanthanum,
was used as a carrier material, they found that the active product was
carried along in a number of chemical operations. But lanthanum was 35
elements removed from uranium! After several trials, lanthanum as a possibility
was discounted.

Now, actinium (element 89) was recognized by chemists to have properties
quite similar to lanthanum. They soon found that the 3-1/2-hour product
differed from actinium. Not completely satisfied that the substance was
lanthanum or actinium, they were forced to place it in the already overcrowded
home of the transuranic elements. However, the 3-1/2-hour product
was actually lanthanum, far down in the periodic table. If they had recognized
this fact, they would have been the discoverers of what was soon to be called
uranium fission.
