It is just as if we were to assign relative values to different chips in a card
game. Give the red chips a value of 10, the blues 50, the yellows 100. In the
same way, the chemist's atomic weight scale is simply a relative one, based on
oxygen as 16.

How many elements? . . .

When compared with the excitement of the hunt for new elements, even
many of the adventures of Sherlock Holmes seem a bit amateurish. The rules
are very simple. Your tools are the ordinary tools of the laboratory. Remember
that atom-smashing machines and other gadgets of our atomic age are strictly
out of bounds here. You can use a gas flame or even an oxyacetylene welding
torch in order to "excite" the various atoms to induce them to make new
combinations.

You have a material X, which you suspect is a new element. How can you
be sure? Using the heat from the torch or another source and trying various
procedures such as mixing with other materials, you attempt to produce material
X from other substances. You also try to convert material X into other
substances. If you can start with 2 pounds of one substance and 3 pounds of
another and get 5 pounds of X, then obviously you have no element but a
compound. Or, if you can start with 5 pounds of X and get 2 pounds of one
material and 3 of another, then X is not an element. But if--no matter what you
do--you can never get X from smaller amounts of other substances, or if you
can never get two or more different materials out of X alone, then you have
an element. If the properties of X are entirely different from those of all other
known elements, then you have a new element.

chemist Mendeleeff helped us out a great deal by setting up a table by
which it was possible to describe the properties of the missing element before
anyone had actually discovered it. The chemist refers to this as the periodic
table. We might look upon it as a rogues' gallery, each drawer of which
contains information about one element. Mendeleeff's table contains 92
