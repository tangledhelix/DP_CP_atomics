Chain reaction possible but not certain . . .

As the Second World War entered its first grim stages, many of the
basic facts about uranium fission were known. All the avenues of experimentation
were now converging on the remaining problems. Would the
secondary neutrons be lost before they could be absorbed by a U235 atom?
How could we increase the number of neutrons that are effective in causing
fission? How should we build a machine to utilize these successive fissions
to give us practical and controllable amounts of energy?

Scientists the world over began to talk seriously about the possibility
of practical atomic-energy machines using U235. But before work on this
momentous phase of atomic science could be undertaken to any extent,
the Second World War had begun. Imaginative authors as well as conservative
scientists began to speculate on the possibility of a superbomb
based on a chain reaction using U235. But by this time a black-out curtain
began to settle on the exchange of vital information that might well become
a decisive element in the war.
