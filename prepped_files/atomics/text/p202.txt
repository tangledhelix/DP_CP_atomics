Would the bomb work? . . .

All these considerations and many others not yet revealed in official documents
had to be taken into account in the preparation of the bomb. Refinement
and yet more refinement of measurements had been necessary. Calculation
after calculation had no doubt been made using these measurements.
Correction for every conceivable error or unusual event had been attempted.
Putting all these measurements, calculations, and corrections together in one
bomb that represented the culmination of 4 years of work on the part of the
top brains of the nation--at an expenditure of 2 billion dollars--placed a great
responsibility upon the leaders of the atomic-bomb scientists.

Would the bomb be a dud? Just what would be the effects? What unknown
dangers to mankind would be revealed as a result of the first tests?

These questions were answered partially on July 16, 1945, when the first
atomic bomb was exploded in New Mexico. Additional information was
provided by the explosions over Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the controlled
tests at Bikini atoll. What did these explosions reveal?
