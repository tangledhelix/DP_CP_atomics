elements are all different. When the atoms of elements combine, molecules
are formed. In separating atoms from each other and inducing them to
form new partnerships, the chemist frequently must supply a certain amount
of energy. It is a familiar fact that a piece of coal is perfectly stable at ordinary
temperatures when in contact with the atmosphere. In order to bring about
the chemical reaction

Carbon -J- oxygen --> carbon dioxide + heat energy

C    O2     CO2

it is first necessary to heat the coal to a sufficiently high temperature. This
ordinary burning of coal is, of course, not a transmutation of elements. Every
carbon atom picks up two partner oxygen atoms, and the resulting product is a
molecule of carbon dioxide. Sometimes the tendency of atoms to combine
together to form molecules is so great that no outside heat need be supplied,
as in the case of the spontaneous combustion of white phosphorus in air.
Occasionally the chemist may use electric energy (supplied from a lead storage
battery, a dry cell, etc.) in order to pull molecules apart into the component
elements. This is called electrolysis. An example would be the electrolysis of
water to produce hydrogen and oxygen--a large-scale commercial process.

The chemist has attempted to obtain high temperatures in the laboratory
in order to effect many chemical reactions. He has progressively developed
heaters or torches that yield higher and higher temperatures. The match,
the candle, the alcohol lamp, the Bunsen gas burner, the plumber's blowtorch,
the oxyhydrogen torch, the atomic hydrogen torch, the electric furnace--all
tended to increase the heat-producing power of the laboratory chemist. Still,
with all these heat producers, he could succeed only in changing the groupings
and arrangements of atoms, but not the atoms themselves. With these devices,
he could obtain temperatures of several thousand degrees--still far too low
for exciting the particles inside the nucleus.
