
Do all gases diffuse at the same rate? This question was raised by the Scotchman
Thomas Graham, working at the University of London early in the
nineteenth century. Simple experiments showed that the amounts of gases
that would diffuse through a tiny hole in a given time varied with the particular
gas used. As might have been expected, lighter gases diffused more
rapidly than heavier gases.

In 1896, Lord Rayleigh suggested that the diffusion method might be used
to separate two gases in a mixture. After diffusing through a porous barrier,
the mixture would be richer in the lighter gas since the faster moving light
molecules would go through the small pores of a barrier more rapidly. However,
this idea did not appear very exciting to the chemist. In the first place,
large volumes of gases would be needed. Further, there usually are simple
chemical methods of separating one gas in a mixture from another. If we wish
to remove the carbon dioxide from a sample of air, we can bubble the air
through a caustic medium, which combines with the carbon dioxide. If we
desire to remove water vapor from the air, we may simply pass the air over
a good drying agent, such as calcium chloride.

However, 17 years later, the suggestion of Lord Ray leigh found a new and
important use. As has been described earlier, J. J. Thomson, F. W. Aston, and
others had revealed early in the twentieth century the existence of isotopes, or
chemical twins, of ordinary stable elements. Mass spectrometer measurements
showed that neon gas contained two isotopes of weight 20 and 22, with about
90 per cent of the neon atoms having weight 20. Separation of these isotopes
by gas diffusion was undertaken.

Minute separation is achieved . . .

In 1913, Aston was able to bring about a very slight separation of the isotopes
by allowing them to diffuse through clay pipestems. In the early twenties
the American physical chemist W. D. Harkins experimented with the separation
of the isotopes of chlorine (35 and 37). He succeeded in obtaining a sample
of chlorine which was slightly enriched with the heavier isotope and with
atomic weight 35.515 instead of 35457 as found in nature. Even obtaining this
slight difference meant a great deal of laboratory effort and time!
