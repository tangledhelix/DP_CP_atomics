
Some savings-bank arithmetic . . .

This multiplication factor, or growth factor, is familiar to us in many
other aspects of living. Compound interest increases in the same manner.
When we say that the bank's rate of interest is i per cent compounded
annually, we mean that the amount of money will increase i per cent each
year over the amount the year before. Since the interest is added each year
to the amount of the previous year, the increase is based upon greater and
greater amounts and the same i per cent of these greater amounts increases
the interest on the money each year.

For example, let us start in the year 1900 with $100 to be compounded
each year at 1 per cent interest. At the end of the first year (1901) we have
$101. (The interest was $1.) Now our base is larger ($101 instead of $100).
The next year (1902) our interest is 1 per cent of $101 and therefore amounts
to $1.01 instead of $1. Our savings now are $102.01. In 1903 we have $103.03.
In 1904 it is $104.06. In 1905 it is $105.10. Note how those pennies keep
creeping up. What starts out as a small amount in the pennies soon rolls
up faster and faster and becomes more and more significant as the years
go by.

How long would it take to double our original deposit? Because the
original amount of interest at 1 per cent was $1, one might be misled
into forecasting that it would take about 100 years to pile up an additional
$100 and thus double the money. Actually, these pennies begin to increase
so fast in later years that it takes about 70 years instead of 100 to double.
But now the base is twice as great as when we started. Our interest is 1
per cent of $200, and the interest earned is $2 in 1971 instead of $1 at the
start. In another 70 years we would double the money again. In the next
70 years our money would double once more. We thus have exactly the same
situation here as with the chain letter, except that the doubling takes place
every 70 years instead of every few days. In another 70 years we would have
$400. The next 70 years $800, then $1,600, $3,200, $6,400 as the 70-year periods
roll by. Just as our chain letter increased from 1 person to 524,288 by the
twentieth round, so the original $100 starting in 1900 would have increased
that many times to $52428,800 by the twentieth round of 70-year periods.
