Furthermore, investigations can be made of the changes that take place
over a period of time. Not only can we tell what the total effect of the introduction
of certain chemicals is, but we can also watch the rate at which the changes
take place, how the material is taken in or let out at various points under
investigation. All this while the radioactive atoms "radio" their presence to
the outside, and we can estimate the amount of substance present by the
strength of the signal sent out.

Radioactive phosphorus as an example of tagging . . .

It has been known for quite some time that phosphorus plays an important
role in the growth of living things. Yet many vital details about the action
of phosphorus have been completely lacking. What happens to the phosphorus
when it is taken into the body? Where does it go? How rapidly? Suppose
that we try to answer these questions by feeding some ordinary phosphorus
in suitable form to an animal. After several hours, we can kill the animal and
test for phosphorus in certain bones. We will of course get a test for phosphorus,
but we cannot tell whether the test came from the phosphorus that was fed
to the animal that day--or the previous month. After all, phosphorus normally
makes up part of the bone structure.

It is here that tagging the phosphorus atom enters the scene. Radioactive
phosphorus 32, with a half-life of about 14 days, can be made by bombarding
sulfur with neutrons from a pile. We can then add a speck of this phosphorus
to some food consumed by an animal or a human being. Thereafter, these
radioactive atoms will serve as excellent spies. They will travel wherever the
stable phosphorus goes throughout the body. Yet their presence can be detected
easily by using the Geiger counter or the electroscope. Perhaps after a
few hours the animal is killed. Radioactive phosphorus is found in the blood
and liver. But if the animal is allowed to live several days after eating the
"spy" sample, it is found that radioactive phosphorus is present mainly in
the bones. Information such as this can furnish vital clues as to the part
played by this element in the functioning of the body.
