
Distillation can be used to concentrate heavy water . . .

Distillation is one of the most frequently practiced techniques of the
laboratory chemist. The rapid growth of our petroleum industry and the
production of organic solvents on a large scale has furnished us with unparalleled
technical know-how in this vital field. Particularly during the
era of Prohibition, many amateurs found this an effective (albeit illegal)
device for separating one liquid from another.

The principle involved in distillation is familiar to many of us. Hold
a cold iron poker in the steam coming from the boiling water in a teakettle
and notice that drops of water have condensed on the poker. These
drops are pure "distilled water."
Now suppose we put some sea water
into the teakettle. The water
that now condenses on the iron
poker or any other cold object is
also pure water. Why? Water boils
at 2120 F. Salt boils only at a very
high temperature. Thus we say
that water is the more volatile of
the two materials. Here we can
make an almost perfect separation
of the salt water into salt and water.

Now let us place in the kettle a
sample of beer that contains about 3 per cent alcohol by weight. Let us further
assume that the beer contains only alcohol and water. Now examine the drops
of liquid that condense on a cold object and you will find by chemical tests that
the condensed liquid is richer in alcohol than the original beer. This occurs because
alcohol is more volatile than water and boils at only 1730 F, while water
boils at 2120 F. The beer will boil somewhere between these two temperatures.
But when the boiling takes place, the molecules of alcohol will boil
off far more rapidly than those of water. Thus, when the vapor is condensed
by cooling it will be found to be richer in alcohol than the original mixture.

Now if we wished to get a sample even richer in alcohol, we could collect
some of the drops that condensed and place them in a smaller teakettle and
distill once more. Of course this would be a tedious job. Therefore, for industrial
purposes, huge towers are constructed in which the simple step of
distillation is repeated many times in one process. As a result, we achieve an
excellent separation of the two liquids that were present in the original solution.
