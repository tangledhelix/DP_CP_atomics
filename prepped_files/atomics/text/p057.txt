But first it was necessary to devise ways and means of detecting and measuring
this new radiation. Devices of the utmost ingenuity were invented to
enable the scientist to see the paths of individual particles and to count them as
they shot out of the radioactive atoms.

One of the most important devices developed for this study was the cloud
chamber invented by C. T. R. Wilson, a British scientist, which actually enabled
us to see the path of a single particle shooting out of the radium. The
instrument is exceedingly simple and makes use of the principles of rain formation
in the air.

Ordinarily, rain droplets form in air whenever it cools below a certain
temperature called the dew point.
For example, water droplets (or
dew) form on the outside of a
pitcher of ice water on a hot summer
day because the water vapor
in the humid air is cooled below the
dew point and condenses on the
cold pitcher. If a closed tank of
water with an air space above it is
permitted to stand for a time, the
air in the space above the water becomes
saturated (full of water
vapor). A very slight cooling
should be sufficient to cause some
of the water vapor to condense.
However, it has been observed that
one more condition is necessary for
the formation of water droplets in
air. Dust particles or electrically
charged air particles must be present to act as centers of formation for the
droplets.
