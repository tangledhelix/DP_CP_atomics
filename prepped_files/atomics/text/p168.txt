This pile was considerably larger than the one at Chicago. It consisted of
a cube of graphite with hollow horizontal channels that were filled with
uranium. The uranium was inserted in the form of cylinders, or "slugs," protected
by airtight casings of aluminum. There was sufficient space between
the uranium slugs and the graphite to permit cooling air to flow. Many
other holes were made in the graphite cube in order to allow controls and
measuring instruments to be inserted. Operation of this pile was started on
November 4, 1943. In a few days it was liberating energy at the rate of 500
kilowatts. Improvements in air circulation soon enabled the pile to operate
satisfactorily at a power level of 800 kilowatts. The surface of the uranium
slugs, as a result of good air cooling, was maintained at about 300° F., well
above the temperature of boiling water. Under these conditions, the pile
operated satisfactorily until the spring of 1944. At that time, several improvements
were made and it became possible to operate at 1,800 kilowatts in
May of 1944.

The most remarkable feature of this pile was the ease of construction and
operation. It operated more smoothly than had been expected by even the
more optimistic workers. Sufficient precautions were taken to protect the
personnel from the radiations that are formed during its operation. Much
valuable information was gained about the chemistry of the products of
uranium fission. The biological effects of the radiations accompanying fission
were studied in order better to protect the health of the operators. It was
possible to pull out some uranium slugs after a time and learn more about
the extraction of the very small quantities of plutonium that were now
present in these "hot" or radioactive slugs. Our gradual progress in the
extraction of plutonium will be described in more detail in a later section.

The Hanford pile--first large-scale plutonium production . . .

There is little doubt that the construction and operation of the Hanford
pile will rank high with the great technological achievements of our age. If
this had been the undertaking of an industrial corporation, the stockholders
would have been perfectly justified in firing the director of research for
attempting an expensive project that had every chance of being a complete
failure. But the urgency of the military situation resulted in the accomplishment
of the impossible.
