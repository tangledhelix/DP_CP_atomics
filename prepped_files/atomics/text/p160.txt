1 atom of U235 out of 140 atoms of uranium. Once this occurred, the U235
would be split open by the slow neutron and it would have accomplished
its mission of producing a new crop of neutrons to keep the chain reaction
going. The slow neutron's careful selectivity for capture by U235 but not
by U238 thus makes a chain reaction possible with ordinary uranium.

The lattice arrangement is therefore ideal for our purpose. Some neutrons
will--even under this arrangement--be captured by U238. Some will
be lost to neutron-robber impurities. Some will escape to the outside. But
with this method we can cut the neutron losses down so much that at least
one out of each crop of several neutrons resulting from a fission will
be effective in causing another fission. Our chain reaction can now proceed.

But many problems remained. In order to construct such a pile, a great
deal of information had to be obtained. How fast are the neutrons from
fission? How many are there? How much space for carbon shall there
be in the lattice? How much uranium shall be placed in one can? How
much will be needed before the critical size is reached where k equals 1 and
the chain reaction proceeds? How pure must the uranium and carbon be
to cut down on losses from neutron robbers? All these questions and more
had to be answered before a pile could be constructed. Experimentation on
these problems was undertaken with great vigor.

In the fall of 1941, Fermi reported a value of 0.87 for k. This was disappointing,
for only 87 new neutrons were produced at the cost of 100 original
neutrons. But it seemed that this was only a beginning estimate; other arrangements
of uranium and graphite could be used and the purity of the
materials could be improved. By May of 1942, uranium of higher purity was
used. The total impurities were now less than 1 per cent and the neutron
robber, boron, was reduced to only a few parts per million. Using these materials,
a value of 0.98 was reported--only 0.02 away from the desired goal. In
June, 1942, sufficiently pure materials were used and a k of 1.007 was reportedl
