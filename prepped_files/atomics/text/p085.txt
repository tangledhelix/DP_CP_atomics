the process of obtaining uranium 235 in the manufacture of the atomic bomb.

In its ability to smash atoms, the cyclotron has many advantages over
naturally radioactive materials. A far more intense beam can be obtained, and
beams of various charged particles can be produced at will. Thus we can use
protons, or deuterons, or helium nuclei and experiment with each of these in
their atom-smashing properties on any desired material. In addition, the energy
can be regulated at will to produce any desired amount of atom-smashing
speed. The use of this machine has produced a wealth of knowledge without
which the development of atomic energy would have been greatly retarded.

What limits the cyclotron? . . .

You have no doubt begun to wonder why only positively charged particles
were speeded up in the cyclotron. Why not do the same with electrons? Theory
shows that such a lightweight particle very soon acquires a velocity practically
that of light itself. The net effect is to make it very difficult to give the electrons
additional "pushes" at just the right time. In fact, this feature seems
to limit the usefulness of the cyclotron for positive particles beyond a certain
energy.

The problem of obtaining very high speed electrons thus remained even
after the cyclotron was invented. In 1941, D. W. Kerst invented a device which
has already produced 100-M.e.v. electrons and shows promise of extension
to several hundred M.e.v. This machine is called the betatron. How does it
work?

High-speed electron gun . . .

The operation of the betatron might be likened, in a crude analogy, to the
hammer throw. In that sport, a weight on the end of a wire is whirled about
faster and faster, picking up speed until the thrower releases the wire and
weight. The electron in a betatron receives similar treatment.
