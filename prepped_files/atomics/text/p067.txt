is unknown to us. It may consist of a great many solid balls packed

How can we tell?

To find the answer, let us shoot thousands of bullets into the box and note
carefully what happens to them. Suppose that every bullet is deflected sharply
or is actually forced to rebound. Our conclusion would be that the material
making up these unknown objects was distributed rather uniformly with few
spaces between objects. On the other hand, suppose that, out of 1,000 bullets,
999 ripped through the cardboard box without showing any deflection whatsoever.
It would then become clear that the matter in each unknown object
must be very highly concentrated in a small region and that the bulk of the
object is simply empty space. Of course, this example is rather crude, but
the same basic principle was applied to the study of atomic architecture.

The first bullets used were high-speed electrons. Later, alpha particles
(helium nuclei) shot out of radioactive atoms proved to be highly successful
artillery. The targets were atoms whose size was known to be about one one-hundred-millionth
of an inch in diameter. That is, ioo million of these atoms
would have to be lined up side by side to measure i inch in length. Or, to put
it in another way, in order to magnify an atom to the size of a penny, the thickness
of a human hair would have to be enlarged to cover more than 20 city
blocks !

Rutherford proposes a solar-system model for the atom . . .

Among the first to carry on this strange target practice was the German
physicist Lenard. He shot high-speed electrons into various materials. To his
surprise, they encountered little resistance and the great majority of the electrons
passed through undetected. Lenard concluded that very few electrons
would have gone through if the atoms were uniformly massive. He reasoned
that the atom consists of a core that is very difficult to penetrate, surrounded
by a diffuse electron cloud.

Rutherford carried on this pioneering work with great vigor and success.
Instead of using electrons, he conceived the idea of using natural heavy
artillery, namely, the alpha particles shot out from radioactive atoms. The
alphas are 7,500 times as heavy as the electrons and therefore should yield more
information about the massive core of the atom--in exactly the same manner
that the resistance of a wall is better gauged by a battering ram than by a
sling shot. Rutherford found that, on the average, an alpha particle would
travel through about 200,000 atoms before suffering a serious deflection.
