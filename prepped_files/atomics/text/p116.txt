Millikan ventures out on a limb . . .

After the war, the American physicist Robert A. Millikan probed higher
into the atmosphere above the earth. He sent up balloons and instruments to
a height of about 10 miles and also made measurements on mountaintops,
in valleys, and underneath the surface of bodies of water. In 1925, he presented
to the world his first conclusions on the nature of these radiations. Some
were highly unorthodox or revolutionary. Indeed, some were considered highly
dubious. But they excited unusual interest and stimulated scientists all over
the world to produce evidence in order to prove or disprove them.

There was universal agreement with Millikan's observations that these
radiations contained some highly penetrating constituents. Most scientists
were also in agreement with his view that these radiations were cosmic in
origin--that they came from the far reaches of interstellar space. Hence we
call these radiations cosmic rays. But Millikan stirred up a hornet's nest of
debate and disagreement when he made the bold assertion that all cosmic rays
consist of high-energy radiation, or photons (akin to the gamma rays and
x-rays), rather than electrified particles. And a storm of controversy was engendered
