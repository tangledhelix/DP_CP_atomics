Calling the roll of additional tracers . . .

Radioactive phosphorus was used in many exploratory studies several yean
before the uranium pile was developed. Phosphorus plays an essential part
in the growth and development of all living cells, both in the plant and animal j
world. Growth of bacteria has been traced with this radioactive isotope
Development of teeth and bone structure will be more thoroughly under- k
stood. Different kinds of soil can be readily tested for phosphorus intake by
plants.

Radioactive sodium can be used to trace the circulation of the blood and the
rate at which salts travel through different parts of the circulatory system.
It has also been used to measure the rate of circulation of the blood in cases j
of "immersion foot" and hardening of the arteries. A strontium isotope, similar
in chemical properties to bone-forming calcium, has been used to study
the healing of bone fractures. The details of insulin formation in the body
can be studied using radioactive zinc and iodine. This should help our understanding
of diabetic conditions. The extremely important functioning of the
thyroid gland and its abnormalities can be impressively explored using radioactive
iodine. Radioactive iron is used as a tracer in finding out the details
about red-cell formation and decay in the blood stream. Some early high lights
along this road are described in the next chapter, which deals with medicine
and atomic energy.

Radioactive isotopes in industry . . .

The usefulness of these tagged atoms extends far beyond the domain
of living things alone. A vast body of knowledge will be gathered about
chemical reactions in general. In many cases the chemist already knows a great
deal about the original materials and the finished products, but he is often
ignorant about the separate steps in the process. Yet a knowledge of these
steps would help immeasurably in improving the process. Tagging some of
the atoms will help to illuminate the in-between stages. Radioactive atoms
can be added to the flowing materials in a large chemical plant. By following
the radioactivity, more accurate knowledge of the flow of materials will be
possible. Thus, it may be desired to trace the molecules of a particular sample
of oil as they traverse the complex flow patterns in a gasoline-refining plant
Or it may be possible to trace the underground flow of oil near oil wells by
introducing radioactive material at one point and noting its motion by instruments.
