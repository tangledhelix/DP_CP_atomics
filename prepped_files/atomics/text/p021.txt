Anarchy inside a gas . . .

During the middle of the nineteenth century many investigators seeking to
understand the nature of heat looked to the properties of gases for more clues.
As a result of the brilliant work of such men as James Joule and James Clerk
Maxwell, based on the facts provided by Rumford, Davy, Brown, and others,
it is now possible for us to describe what is going on inside a gas even though
the actual molecules have never been seen.

If we were able to see the molecules in a quart bottle of oxygen gas, we
would be amazed by the chaos and confusion. Inside the bottle are over 25,000
billion billion molecules of oxygen. The molecules are extremely small. In
fact, we could line up 100 million of them along a length of 1 inch.

We are most impressed by their movements. The molecules are rushing
about in all directions with speeds of almost 1,000 miles per hour. In moving
around they make repeated collisions with their neighbors; in fact, every
molecule makes on the average 10 billion collisions per second! As the molecules
strike the walls of the container and bounce off, they create a considerable
pressure against the wall.

us is exerting a pressure of about 15 pounds over each square inch of our body
surface--equivalent to carrying a weight of about a ton on our heads and
