In Chapter Ten the properties of the valuable atomic projectile, the deuteron,
were described. We were there introduced
to the brilliant experiments
of Prof. Harold C. Urey and
his coworkers that led to the discovery
of deuterium, or heavy hydrogen,
for which he was awarded
the Nobel prize in chemistry in
1934. This isotope of hydrogen is
exactly twice as heavy as ordinary
hydrogen, since it contains a neutron
in addition to a proton in the
nucleus. At the time of its discovery
there was no reason to predict that this laboratory curiosity was to play an
important role in atomic energy.

Heavy hydrogen finds important use as the source of deuteron beams in
the cyclotron. In the form of heavy water it is of great value in biological
"tagging" experiments and also as
a moderator in a uranium-graphite
pile. To distinguish this molecule
of water from one of ordinary water,
the chemist writes the formula
D2O instead of H2O. In this formula
the D stands for heavy hydrogen,
deuterium.

There are only slight differences
in properties between ordinary water
and heavy water. It is approximately
10 per cent heavier; it
freezes at 38.8° F instead of 32° F; and it boils at 214.6° F instead of 212° F.

It is rather rare. Only 1 molecule in 5,000 of water is heavy water. This
amounts to about 1 drop in a glass.
