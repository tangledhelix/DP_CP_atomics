If nothing else were available, we could use the high-speed particles shot
out of radioactive materials, but this is a very meager source. As a result, the
laboratory worker has invented machines in which charged particles can be
speeded up to many thousands of miles per second. These machines are frequently
referred to as atom smashers. In this group are included the cyclotron,
the electrostatic generator, and the betatron. Additional ingenious machines
invented very recently include the linear accelerator and the synchrotron.

Measuring projectile energies--the electron volt . . .

How shall we express the energies of these projectile particles? It would
be nice to use a practical unit, such as calories or kilowatt-hours. But the current
newspaper headlines dealing with atomic discoveries have practically
forced our decision. A news item will report the successful production of "a
twenty million volt proton," a "one hundred million volt electron" and will say
that "the splitting of the uranium atom sets free particles of one hundred
million volts." If the reports say this, then they are of course speaking loosely,
for they must mean "twenty million electron volt protons," "a one hundred
million electron volt electron," etc. What, then, is an electron volt?

The volt is a familiar enough word to all of us. We speak of the no volts
used in our homes. Or we may have occasion to refer to the 6 volts supplied
by a storage battery, or to the 2,000-volt electric chair. The general idea behind
all these numbers is that the higher the voltage, the greater the strength of the
electric current produced by it, other things being equal. All of us know that
high voltage will give a greater shock than low voltage under similar conditions.
This idea of voltage is rather approximate but with slight modification
will serve for our purpose.

A charged particle "falling through" a voltage applied between two plates
resembles a stone falling under the force of gravity. In each case, the charged
particle or the stone is being speeded up by the application of attractive forces.
If a higher voltage is used it is equivalent to placing a stronger positive and
negative charge on the plates, and the force of attraction will be increased.
The charged particle will therefore move across the tube faster and gather up
more energy as it strikes the plate. Thus the objective in atom smashers is
to place as high a voltage as possible into the machine so as to get the maximum
push (or pull) on the electric particles and thereby give them the greatest
possible speed and energy.
