Let us assume that the air molecules from one single breath scattered to
the four winds and spread into the atmosphere of the earth so that they were
distributed evenly into every portion of air in our globe. There would be
enough molecules from that one breath to supply 1 molecule for every other
breath that anyone else might take, anywhere on the earth.

If we represented each one of these uranium atoms by a ball the size of a
period (.) on this page, then the atoms in a pound of uranium would fill a box

about 15 miles high, wide, and long. Such an enormous cube could contain
millions of buildings the size of the Empire State Building!

What is the structure of the atom? . . .

The enormous advance toward the solution of the problem of the structure
of the atom which has been recorded in this chapter was soon to be dwarfed
by even more startling discoveries and brilliant observations.

Thus far, we have received most of our evidence from the particles making
up the cathode rays and positive rays. These, however, have the well-known
properties of matter, such as weight, energy of motion, etc. But evidence
toward the unveiling of atomic secrets was also coming from still another
direction--from the study of light.

We shall find that light also illuminated the atom.
