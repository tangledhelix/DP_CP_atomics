But to go one step further, this amount of charge on the nucleus is the important
and distinguishing factor about the atom. In fact, the properties of
the elements are determined by this positive charge. If two atoms have the same
positive charge on the nucleus, then they must be atoms of the same element
and must have practically identical properties, even though they have different
weights. This positive charge on the nucleus is so important that we
generally identify the element today simply by referring to its atomic number,
(equal to the positive charge on the nucleus). Thus hydrogen, atomic number
1, has a positive charge of 1 on the nucleus. Helium, atomic number 2,
has 2 positive charges. Lead, atomic number 82, has 82 such charges.

This identification of the elements by number cleared up a puzzling problem
that had baffled chemists for many years. From the very beginnings of
careful measurements of atomic weights, the chemist had attempted to arrange
the elements in some way so as to discover possible relationships between
them. A natural arrangement was by atomic weight. True enough, a strong
similarity was noticed between elements in certain groups when arranged
in that manner (our familiar rogues' gallery of the elements). But there were a
number of cases in which the arrangement according to atomic weight led
to results so absurd that a heavier atom had to be put ahead of a lighter atom.
This was very annoying, since even a few exceptions indicate that the theory
may be radically incorrect. Here the use of atomic number was a great boon.
The annoying exceptions disappeared completely. In addition, Moseley's
brilliant work showed us at least one sure method of determining the atomic
number of an element. This has helped considerably in the search for some
of the very rare elements of the rogues' gallery.

How many planetary electrons? . . .

Moseley's work furnished a definite answer to the question of planetary
electrons also. An atom is electrically neutral--there is no excess of positive
or negative charges. As a result, the number of planetary electrons in a neutral
atom must equal the number of positive charges on the nucleus, namely, the
atomic number. Thus, using the examples above, hydrogen has 1 planetary
electron, helium 2 planetary electrons, and lead 82 planetary electrons.
