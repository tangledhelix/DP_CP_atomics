be produced in the future.

Deuteron beams were also found to be very effective in producing artificially
radioactive elements. The deuteron consists of a proton and a neutron.
The charge might be looked upon as a handle by which the deuteron can be
given a high velocity in a cyclotron or Van de Graaff generator. Intense beams
of deuterons of high velocity can thus be obtained.

Up to the early forties neutron sources were so weak that it was frequently
found more satisfactory to use deuteron rather than neutron projectiles. An
example of this was the production of radioactive sodium by bombarding
ordinary table salt with deuterons. As a result, a radioactive chemical twin
of sodium is formed with a half-life of about 15 hours. This of course represented
a great step forward, even before the days of large-scale atomic energy.
A single day's bombardment of salt with deuterons in the cyclotron beam
produced radioactive sodium equal in gamma-ray production to about $10,000
worth of radium. Of course, the salt did not really have that value, for its
radiation was cut down to a very small amount after a number of weeks, while
radium retains its radiation power for thousands of years.

Gamma rays and high-energy photons have also been used as atomic projectiles
in helping to produce small amounts of radioelements.

The successful operation of large-scale atomic-energy machines (described
in a later chapter) has provided us with neutron sources many thousands of
times more powerful than had hitherto been attainable. Since the neutron is
the ace projectile for atomic bombardments, it has now become possible for
us to make enormously greater quantities of artificially radioactive atoms.
Many of us look upon this fact as one of the greatest boons of the atomic age
into which we have lately penetrated. In Chapters Twenty-four and Twenty-five
we shall devote some time to the immense possibilities foreshadowed by
this abundance of radioactive atoms.
