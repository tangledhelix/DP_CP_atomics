Atomic "tracer" methods are used . . .

A special "tracer" technique was used in the solution of this problem. The
chemical behavior of an atom depends upon the charge on the nucleus, or the
atomic number. If an atom is unstable (radioactive) this will in no way affect
the ordinary chemical behavior of the atom. The chemist can perform his
analysis on a material with full confidence that all radioactive isotopes of any
element will act like other atoms of the same element and will be carried
along with them in all ordinary chemical and physical processes that he may
apply.

Thus, let us suppose that we suspect the presence of a radioactive isotope
of lead (atomic number 82) in the uranium that was bombarded with neutrons.
We cannot test this material directly for lead because the number of
atoms involved is far too small to be detected by ordinary methods. We therefore
add some ordinary lead to the uranium and then apply well-known
chemical procedures to remove it. If any radioactive atoms of lead are present
they will trail along with the added lead through all the chemical steps
applied. If the final lead product contains the radioactivity, as indicated by a
Gciger counter or other instrument, then it is clear that some of the radioactivity
originally present in the uranium was due to lead atoms. On the other
