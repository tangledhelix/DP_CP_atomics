We are also surprised to notice the materials that were used in the uranium-carbon
power plant. In building a house or a ship, we naturally look for
materials having the desired mechanical properties. In the case of an airplane,
we look for lighter metals. But we are limited to a greater extent in searching
for materials used inside the chain-reaction space. Neutron-robber material
which stops the chain reaction cannot be used. Lead and bismuth absorb
neutrons to a negligible extent and, as a result, they might be used as circulating
liquids since they are moderately low-melting materials. Iron is a neutron
robber and its use therefore is limited. Aluminum, silicon, and magnesium
could be tolerated in considerable quantities, however.

Is there enough uranium on earth? . . .

Uranium is far from being a rare element. Some estimates indicate that
it is about as plentiful as copper, twice as plentiful as zinc, and four times as
plentiful as lead. The 1939 price of about $2 a pound for unpurified uranium
is another indication of the fact that it is far from being a scarce material. The
same applies to the other common fissionable element, thorium, which may
be used along with uranium as a source of atomic energy. Thorium is about
three times as plentiful as uranium. Before the days of electric lighting, thorium
oxide was used as the glowing element of gas mantles. Thus, there seems to be
no particular difficulty about obtaining either of these two atomic-energy fuels.

Sources of uranium ores are rather widespread, having been reported in
Argentina, Australia, the Belgian Congo, Brazil, Canada, Czechoslovakia
Chile, China, England, India, Madagascar (France), Mexico, Norway, the
Philippines, Poland, Portugal, Russia, South Africa, Sweden, and the United
States. But the known deposits contain only about one five-billionth of the
estimated uranium in the earth's crust. The uranium 235 in the earth's crust is
estimated to be about one-quarter that of coal! But whereas the coal supply
would last us about 3,000 years at present rates of energy usage (assuming all
our energy came from coal), the potential uranium 235 supply would last
about 2 billion years. But if we desired to get all our energy from U235 in
known deposits, it might not even last us a year. All the more reason to conserve
our uranium resources and utilize them only for the most desirable peaceful
purposes.
