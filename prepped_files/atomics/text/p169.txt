methods had advantages as well as severe disadvantages. At first there
had been agreement in favor of a pile in which the hot uranium slugs would
be cooled by helium gas. But, in general, a gas is not an efficient cooling agent,
hence the scale at which such a pile could be operated would be considerably
lowered. It was finally decided to use water cooling, but uranium metal is
attacked by water. This brought up new problems.

Because of the danger of deadly radiations, it was necessary to design the
pile in such a way that it would be possible to pull out uranium slugs
remote control in order to extract the plutonium that had been created in
them. The kind of piping that was to be used had to be chosen with great
care, for the atoms of many metals have the property of robbing excessive
numbers of neutrons from the pile and thus stopping the chain reaction.
Furthermore, it was not known at the time how the graphite and uranium
would hold up mechanically under the enormous bombardment of neutrons
and gamma radiation present in a large pile. Uranium is a very reactive metal;
hence it was necessary to protect the uranium slugs from the cooling water by
a protective layer or coating of some other metal. In addition, the water supply
necessary to cool the pile was comparable to that used by a fair-sized city.

The problem of coping with the highly lethal radiations that are given off
during the operation of this pile was very pressing. These radiations are
strong enough to make it impossible for the operators to go near the pile.
Neutrons or other radiations would tend to escape through small cracks in
barriers that might be erected. Hence, there would probably have to be thick
walls of absorbing material, such as concrete or steel. These radiation shields
would have to be airtight, since air exposed to the radiations from the pile
would become radioactive and highly dangerous to the operators. But the
construction of these protective radiation barriers would necessarily hinder
the removal of hot slugs, the loading of fresh slugs, and the passage of the
water supply.

A site was finally chosen on the Columbia River in a sparsely populated
section in the state of Washington. There would be plenty of cooling water
available since the Columbia River is one of the finest sources of pure cold
river water in the entire country. Further, the site was located near the
Grand Coulee hydroelectric power line, so that there would be ample power to
be utilized in the gigantic construction task ahead. Eventually, an area of
nearly 1,000 square miles was brought under government control. Ground was
broken for the construction on April 6,1943. During the peak of construction,
