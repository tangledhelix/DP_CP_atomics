dioxide gas through the leaves and in the presence of sunlight combine these
simple materials into life-sustaining substances. It appears that certain green

a great deal of study, the chemist does not yet understand how all this occurs,
and he certainly has not been able to duplicate it. Of course, being able to
solve this last problem carries with it some enticing features, including the
ability to manufacture food material without having to grow it.

Several years ago this problem was studied in the laboratory, using radioactive
carbon in the form of carbon dioxide. The early experiments had to
use short-lived carbon 11 with a half-life of only 21 minutes. The plants studied
were given water and radioactive carbon dioxide and were illuminated. After
a time, the amount of fresh carbohydrate formed was measured by finding
out how radioactive the plant carbohydrate had become. These were only
preliminary studies and the problem remained far from solved. Now long-lived
carbon 14 is available for these studies. Plants can be grown using
radioactive carbon dioxide and water containing radioactive hydrogen
(tritium) of atomic weight 3. With the use of these perfect spies we can now
look for great advances in this field.

It is no exaggeration to estimate that literally thousands of investigators will
cut into this puzzling problem using tagged atoms. Is it any wonder then
that the use of tagged atoms in biology is considered as great a step forward
as was the invention of the microscope?

Stable isotopes can also be used . . .

We have thus far been discussing the use of radioactive tracers. In many
experiments we may look for the extremely important use of stable (nonradio-
active) isotopes such as heavy hydrogen and carbon 13. There is obviously
a limit to the amount of radioactive material that a living plant (or animal)
can absorb with safety. But far greater quantities of stable isotopes can be
used without affecting the normal functioning of the living organism.

Thus, it will be possible to grow plants in materials containing high concentrations
of heavy water and carbon 13. The presence of these atoms can
also be detected but not quite as easily as with radioactive atoms. In this case
it is necessary to take a portion of the material of the plant or animal to be
tested and to analyze it in a mass spectrometer, which separates and measures
the amount of isotope present.
