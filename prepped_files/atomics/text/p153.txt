outlined above. The only difference produced by having a small k number
to start with is that it will take a longer time to double. As soon as the
factor exceeds 1, then growth starts. If the rate of growth is maintained
without change, then we have an explosive type of growth.

On the other hand, if the k number is less than 1, then the reverse situation
obtains and there is a decrease or decay instead of growth. Thus, if you
decided to spend 1 per cent of your $100 each year, you would find a gradually
decreasing amount remaining. In this case you are left with 99 per cent of
your money each year as compared with the previous year. The k factor is
now 0.99. Now your fund decays and eventually dies out.

If the k number is exactly 1, then your fund maintains itself without
change. In this case there is neither increase or decrease. The fund remains
fixed.

The growth and decay of population follows a similar pattern. Every
couple must on the average replace itself with two new persons if the race
is to survive. In this case the k number is 1. Two people replace two people.
The population remains stationary. If the k number is greater than 1, that is,
if two people replace themselves with three people, on the average, then the
population increases. If the k number is less than 1, and on the average two
people replace themselves with fewer than two people, then the population
ultimately dies out.

Increasing the neutron population in the atomic bomb . . .

The problem of the release of atomic energy is similar to the problems
of growth of money under compound interest or of growth or decay of
population. But in this case we deal with neutron population. For the sake
of comparison let us assume that we start with 100 atoms that are undergoing
fission (or splitting) and releasing neutrons (similar to our $100 in
the bank). We have already mentioned that when each atom "fisses" (commonly
pronounced "fishes") a number of neutrons are ejected from the
splitting atom at great speed. The exact number is not revealed in the
Smyth report, which simply mentions that it ranges from 1 to 3. Let us
assume here for the sake of our discussion that on the average each U235
atom split by neutron collision produces 3 new high-speed neutrons.
