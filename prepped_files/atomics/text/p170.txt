Plutonium by remote control . . .

We have seen how a small portion of the uranium is converted to plutonium
while the pile operates. The problem now is to separate the small amounts
of plutonium from the uranium. This differs markedly from the problem of
separating U235 from U238, which is mainly the concern of the physicist. The
separation of the isotopes of uranium is made extremely difficult by the fact
that there is virtually no difference in chemical properties of the atoms of
U238 and U235. Maximum exploitation of the very slight difference in weight
of the atoms must therefore be undertaken.

But in the case of plutonium we have an entirely new element, which differs
from uranium in both chemical and physical properties. It thus becomes
possible to separate the plutonium using simple chemical procedures, in very
much the same manner that Marie Curie separated radium and polonium from
uranium and thorium.

When the chemist is presented with the problem of separating a new
element from a given material, he first finds out what the chemical and
physical properties of the material involved are, so that he may utilize these
differences to cause the separation. Once these different properties are known,
the chemist proceeds to separate the metals by a series of chemical and physical
steps. These include such methods as dissolving in various reagents (such
as acids), precipitation of powders from the solution, heating, crystallization,
whirling in centrifuges, distillation, etc. These and other similar methods are
the ones that a chemist would apply to the separation of plutonium from
uranium.

But the problem is complicated in this case by the fact that nobody can
approach the pile or its contents while it is operating because of the intensity
of the radioactivity. This radioactivity persists in the uranium-plutonium
slug even after it has been removed from the pile. The entire chemical and
physical separation must therefore be performed by remote control. No human
hand may touch the materials at any time during the separation process. In
fact, even a close approach for examination is too risky to be permitted.
