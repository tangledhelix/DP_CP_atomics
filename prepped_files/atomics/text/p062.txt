If radium is spontaneously decomposing into radon with the emission
an alpha particle, it is clear that the radium is gradually disappearing. How
long does this proceed? Does radium stop committing suicide in this manner
after a fraction of the original has disappeared? Or does it decay in a continu-
manner according to some law? If so, what is the law governing radioactive
decay?

The answers to these questions were eagerly sought. As a result of many
experiments, they proved to be quite simple, similar to the mortality tables
of the insurance experts.

Life insurance tables for radium . . .

We all know that mortality rates may vary from year to year as a result of
plagues, famine, wars, remarkable advances in medicine, etc. Nevertheless, it
is still possible for a life insurance firm, dealing with a very large clientele,
to base insurance rates on a definite table and to be able to predict, within
a reasonable safety factor, the average life expectancy for an individual in a
definite age bracket. But the statistics governing the probable life period
of any given radium atom are far simpler than any mortality table. True
enough, some radioactive elements decay so rapidly that less than i per cent
of the original quantity may be left after a few minutes; others so slowly
that more than half may still be present after billions of years. But for
a specific radioactive element, there is a definite number that expresses its
chance of survival.

Let us consider the case of radium. The entire mortality table for this element
is summarized in the simple statement that 1/2,300 of any sample of
radium will have disappeared every year, giving of! alpha particles and ending
up as atoms of the gas radon. A very convenient way of expressing the
survival probability is by use of the expression known as half-life, which represents
the time required for one-half the original element present to undergo
disintegration and form a new element. This term half-life might be compared
with the meaning of life expectancy in insurance parlance. The life
expectancy of a person born in the United States is about sixty-five years. This
means that half the people born will reach the age of sixty-five. In the case of
radium its life expectancy, or half-life, is 1,590 years. Half the atoms will have
disintegrated in that time.
