Looking for "fingerprints" with the spectroscope . . .

It was therefore necessary to look for this heavy hydrogen using other types
of measurements. One possible method of attack was to use the spectroscope.
has already been pointed out that atoms give off light when sufficiently
excited. The different frequencies in this light appear as lines in the spectrum
and furnish very accurate fingerprints of the atom. By theory, lines produced
by atoms of heavy hydrogen, should be very close to those of ordinary light
hydrogen. Furthermore, these slight differences could be accurately calculated
from theory.

This obvious challenge was taken up by the American investigators Urey,
Murphy, and Brickwedde in 1932. An ordinary laboratory spectroscope would
be useless here, for the differences could not be detected. They used a 21-foot
instrument called a diffraction grating, which enables finely separated spectrum
Unes to be readily recorded. Using ordinary hydrogen, they observed extremely
faint lines close to the normal, intense hydrogen spectrum. In order
to be able to make a definite conclusion, it was necessary to attempt to enrich
the sample with respect to the heavy isotope. How could this be accomplished?
If heavy hydrogen did exist in small quantities, it was very
probable that it would boil at a slightly higher temperature than ordinary
hydrogen. In other words, if a liquid mixture of light and heavy hydrogen
were permitted to evaporate, the light hydrogen would evaporate more
rapidly and the residue should then be richer in the heavy hydrogen.
