How did this unit package compare with the charge that had been measured
in an approximate way for the electrons in the empty tube? It had the same
value! In other words, the electron represents the unit package of negative
electricity. It is indeed a very small package. We know today that current
flowing through a wire simply represents the flow of electrons. Each second,
5 billion billion of these electrons are flowing through the filament of an ordinary
glowing 100-watt bulb.

An electron weighs very little . . .

Thomson and others had very carefully measured the charge on the electron
divided by the weight. Now, using Millikan's value for the charge, the weight
could also be reliably calculated. To state the weight in ounces would simply
mean writing about 28 ciphers after the decimal point. We'll simply state
that 30,000 trillion trillion of these would have to be added together to register
a weight of 1 ounce. Indeed, this weight is so infinitesimally small that if we
could have 1 electron for every heartbeat of every one of the 2 billion persons
now living on this earth for his entire lifetime and for 30 billion generations
thereafter, we would have accumulated enough to make about 1 ounce c
electrons.

Positive rays in the empty tube . . .

was pointed out in the preceding chapter that matter as a whole is
electrically neutral. Up to this point, we have seen how streams of negative
particles--electrons--were extracted from the negative terminal of the now-

familiar glass tube and caused to stream over to the positive terminal through
the empty space between the two terminals. But what about particles of

positive electricity? If matter as a whole is neutral, and if positive charges
