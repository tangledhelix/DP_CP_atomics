The first uranium heavy-water chain-reacting pile . . .

The advantages of heavy water as a moderator in a chain-reacting uranium
pile excited considerable interest. It is more effective than graphite in slowing
down neutrons and also has less tendency to rob neutrons. As a result, a pile
using heavy water as a moderator can be made more compactly than an
equivalent carbon-uranium pile. But it would have required 2 years to produce
enough heavy water for a fair-sized pile producing plutonium. It was
therefore decided to curtail the program and to limit production to a 250-
kilowatt pile located at the Argonne site near Chicago. Although a "laboratory"
pile, its successful operation marks another pioneer step in the story
of atomic energy. The fascinating details of this atomic-energy machine
taken from the Smyth report, are given below:

"Perhaps the most striking aspect of the uranium and heavy-water pile at
the Argonne is its small size. Even with its surrounding shield of concrete it
is relatively small compared to the uranium-graphite piles.

"By May 15, 1944, the Argonne uranium and heavy-water pile was ready
for test. With the uranium slugs in place, it was found that the chain reaction
in the pile became self sustaining when only three fifths of the heavy water had
been added. The reactivity of the pile was so far above expectations that it
would have been beyond the capacity of the control rods to handle if the remainder
of the heavy water had been added. To meet this unusual and pleasant
situation some of the uranium was removed and extra control rods were
added.

"With these modifications it was possible to fill the tank to the level planned.
By July 4, 1944, W. H. Zinn reported that the pile was running satisfactorily
at 190 kw, and by August 8,1944, he reported that it was operating at 300 kw.
