us large desert areas in the interior of which there appears to be scant life-giving
water for man, animals, and crops. Might it not be possible with atomic
power to evaporate great quantities of water from surrounding seas, condense
the resulting fresh water, and pump it to these desert regions? Industrial
civilizations might spring up in regions so powered, regions which would
otherwise lack coal, water, and fuel. Given this greater area for productive
life, some of the intense competition between peoples struggling amid inadequate
surroundings might be alleviated.

Large power plants using uranium fuel could be set up near the site of desired
projects and these atomic-energy machines could be used to supply compressed
air, steam, and electric power to steam shovels, railroad cars, etc. In this
way it would be easier to mold the surface of the earth to our liking. But some
more imaginative planners, becoming impatient at the pace of controlled
power, suggest using atomic bombs directly. In this way they would blast
mountains in their path, dig giant canals compared to which the Panama
Canal would resemble a ditch, attempt to change the weather in large portions
of the earth by blasting and melting the gigantic icecap in the polar
regions, even attempt to deviate the path of a hurricane.

Atomic energy in industry . . .

Reputable scientists have suggested that atomic energy might be used
to solve that long-sought problem, the manufacture of diamonds. From a
chemical standpoint the problem is easy to state. Simply rearrange the carbon
atoms in a lump of coal or graphite! Unlike many other synthetic marvels
of the chemist, this problem has eluded solution because extremely high
temperatures and pressures are needed. Following one suggestion that has
been made, a huge cavern inside a mountain could be packed with carbon
and an atomic bomb exploded at just the right distance. The intense heat and
pressure might liquefy the carbon. As it cooled this liquid carbon would freeze
and diamonds might result. Should all this come to pass, the value of diamonds
as precious jewels would obviously sink. But diamonds would continue to
have unexcelled value in various industrial uses.

Suggestions have been made that atomic bombs will find use in mining
operations where the rich ore is many feet below the surface or in cases
where the ore is needed in a finely crushed form. Here too the arguments in
favor of "atomic-bomb mining" will have to be very persuasive before we
abandon easily controllable chemical explosives.
