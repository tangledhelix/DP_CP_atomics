
The British physicist F. W. Aston made many outstanding discoveries using
mass spectrometers, which he helped to perfect. Today, over 300 atoms of
different mass have been sorted out and examined.

Let us assume that we are studying the isotopes of neon gas, familiar to us
as a component of the neon sign. Neon gas consists, in the main, of two kinds
of atoms, of weights 20 and 22. The neon gas is introduced into the so-called
mass spectrometer tube at low pressure. Electrons are allowed to collide with
the neon atoms, producing positively charged neon ions of weight 20 and 22.
Under the influence of the attraction of a negatively charged plate, these
positive ions are now induced to move down the tube. After traveling about
12 inches, the ions are now forced to swerve by a magnetic field. Here the
sorting begins to take place. The ions of weight 22 are not swerved so sharply
as those of the lighter neon atom of weight 20. As a result of careful design,
the two beams of ions, 20 and 22, are now separated from each other by a
measurable distance.

How are the separate beams identified? We may allow them to fall on a
photographic plate, producing sharp lines where they fall. From the distances
between these lines, we can accurately determine their atomic weights. It is
more common today actually to measure the tiny current of ions in each
beam and arrive at the weights and relative amounts from the currents that
are observed. These currents are extremely small when compared to a commonly
used electric current. For example, the mass spectrometer current
would have to be multiplied over a million billion times in order to begin to
equal the current through your toaster or flatiron.

Our ability to sort out and weigh various atoms in this useful instrument
has enormously increased our confidence in the reality of atoms and of the
atomic models which we have projected. Today, the mass spectrometer is a
standard tool in the laboratories of many industrial firms as well as universities.
Later we shall see how this method was boldly applied on a several-hundred-million-dollar
scale in order to separate one isotope of uranium from
another.

Armed now with evidence that the core of the atom, the nucleus, is the seat
of transmutations and energies far greater than those obtainable from chemical
reactions, attention was centered on the next important problem of breaking
into this core and finding out its secrets.
