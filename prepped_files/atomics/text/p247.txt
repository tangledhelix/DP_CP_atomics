By 1940, close to 400 radioactive types of atoms had been produced and the
number was continuing to increase.

Now the chain-reacting pile has begun to shoulder the burden of producing
these atoms in greater numbers for the use of a far greater number of investigators.
In two respects the products of the pile cannot be surpassed by the
atom-smashing machines. In the first place, the destruction of the uranium
atom results, as we have seen, in the formation of numerous radioactive fission
products. Included in these are some elements that are relatively scarce in
nature. Second, the chain reaction in the pile is carried by neutrons ranging
in energy up to about 1 M.e.v. So it is possible to obtain neutron beams with
the pile that are many thousands of times more intense than those obtainable
with the cyclotron.

The pile therefore is more useful for making radioactive atoms by means
of neutron bombardment with projectiles having these energies. Fortunately,
several very important isotopes can be made in this manner. Of these, most
scientists will agree that carbon 14 is of greatest value, since the chemistry of
all processes connected with living things involves compounds of carbon.
Scientists consider the importance of this isotope of carbon so great that the
construction and operation of uranium-carbon piles solely for the production
of this material would be amply justified. Some even consider the possibility
that new information gleaned from researches with carbon 14 might someday
outweigh the importance of the discovery of fission itself.

Using "tagged" atoms as tracers . . .

To understand the use of radioactive atoms as tracers, or "spies," we must
keep two things in mind. First, the radioactive twins are easily identified by
the use of simple electrical instruments. Consider a sample of food containing
radioactive carbon that will be used to study a step in digestion. Almost all
the molecules contain ordinary stable carbon atoms. A very few contain
carbon 14. This radioactive atom ejects a beta particle which can create ionization
inside a Geiger counter tube or help discharge a sensitive electroscope.
