
In the first place, we must bear in mind that this actually happens to a considerable
degree in many materials. All liquids evaporate to some extent into
the air. The rapid motion of the molecules causes a certain percentage of the
faster ones to break away from whatever is holding them in place and fly off.
Thus water evaporates into the air. Volatile materials such as gasoline and
ether evaporate much more rapidly, and the presence of their molecules can
readily be detected from the odors they cause.

This process is noticeable in solids as well as liquids. Moth balls disappear
when placed in a closet, leaving only a telltale odor in the air of the closet.
Snow evaporates from the ground and gradually disappears in the wintertime,
even without rain or melting to hasten the process. In this case the
molecules of water (as snow) have gone off into the air as odorless water
vapor. Solid carbon dioxide, or dry ice, does the same. In all cases heating
hastens this process of disappearance of the molecules of substances into the
air, because it increases the motion of the molecules and their tendency to
fly off.

But still the puzzle remains. If molecules moving at great speed have this
tendency of flying off into space in all directions, what keeps solids and liquids
together? Why doesn't everything just evaporate into the air?

Like many other puzzles in science, the clues were provided in most unexpected
ways. It happened that the answer to this question was provided
by the study of electricity.

Again the Greek had a word for it . . .

More than 25 centuries ago in ancient Greece the philosopher Thales
described a most peculiar action of amber after it had been rubbed on other
materials. It had the interesting property of attracting small bits of cloth, hair,
sawdust, etc. Every schoolboy has at one time or other repeated this experiment
by rubbing a bakelite fountain pen on the sleeve of his jacket and attracting
small bits of paper. In fact, the word electricity is taken from the Greek word
for amber, which Thales used in this first electrical experiment.

Scientific discovery moved at a snail's pace from the time of Thales through
the Middle Ages and up to the time of the Renaissance. But with the accelerated
pace of discovery in the sixteenth, seventeenth, and eighteenth centuries
a great many clues were uncovered. Space does not permit us to cite
the many contributions to this important work. All we can do here is to give
the basic conclusions resulting from the study of Thales's experiment.

It was found that all materials showed this property of attracting small
objects after being rubbed. Some materials were better for this purpose than
others, but there were no exceptions in the ability to show this effect.
