atom as a whole must be electrically neutral, it follows that the number of

the equal and opposite charges on the core and the planetary system cancel

If we were to stop here, our story of atomic energy would also have to be
cut short. To the chemist who deals in combinations of atoms to form molecules,
the important facts about the atom are concerned with the nuclear
charge, in other words, the number of electrons revolving about the nucleus.
These will determine all the chemical reactions ordinarily observed on this
earth. But far greater rewards, including the liberation of large-scale atomic
energy and transmutations, await those who are not satisfied with this picture.
Now that we have determined the structure of the atom itself
goes one step farther, to the structure of the nucleus itself. How is the nucleus
put together? What particles can be found inside the "center" of the atom?
Once again the study of radioactive substances was called upon to supply
major clues in answering these questions.

Natural alchemy in terms of the atomic number . . .

What element is formed when radium ejects an alpha particle? A bit of
elementary arithmetic easily furnishes the answer. Radium is element number
88 in the chemist's table, arranged
according to atomic number.
The core of the radium atom
therefore has 88 positive charges.
An alpha particle shooting out of
the nucleus has 2 positive charges.
As a result, the core that remains
has only 86 positive charges. In
other words, the new element
formed should be number 86. This
element is radon, an inert gas from
the chemical point of view, but also
radioactive.
