What could have caused these radiations? Why did they pass through
solid objects? How could they be used? Scientists began to attack these questions
with great vigor. Their researches led inevitably to new knowledge, not
only about the radiations themselves, but about the atoms in the tube which
were emitting these rays. To understand these radiations, we must go back
a bit to look at the many facts which had been uncovered with regard to
the nature of light.

Early discoveries in light . . .

The study of light began long before there was any definite proof of the
existence of atoms. The ancient Greeks had speculated on the nature of the
things we see in flames and in the sun. Some Greek philosophers thought that
we were able to see because certain invisible tentacles stretched out from the
eye. Others thought that a luminous object was shooting off particles which
gave us the sensation of sight.

Throughout the Middle Ages knowledge about light was gathered at a
snail's pace. With the coming of the Renaissance and the rebirth of the quest
for knowledge this accumulation became more rapid. Lenses came into
general use and were soon applied to the construction of telescopes for peering
into the heavens. One of the first fruits of this study was a measurement of
the speed of light by the Danish astronomer Roemer in the seventeenth century.
In order to explain a puzzling observation dealing with the eclipses of
a moon of the planet Jupiter, Roemer came to the conclusion that the speed
of light was 192,000 miles per second. Later measurements showed that the
speed at which light travels is close to 186,000 miles per second.

This speed is so enormous that a plane traveling at that rate could go around
the earth at the equator in less than Yi second--or not much more time than
it takes to wink an eye.

Another important fact noted in these early investigations that was to
play a prominent part in atomic theory was the formation of a rainbow-colored
spectrum whenever a ray of white light was passed through a triangular
prism. The white light broke up into a series of colors, which started
with red at one end and gradually shaded into orange, yellow, green, blue,
indigo, and violet. This experiment was ultimately to become the basis of the
spectroscope, indispensable tool of the chemist and physicist.

What is light? . . .

The many facts gathered up to the middle of the nineteenth century gave
rise to theories to explain them just as naturally as rabbits produce offspring.
