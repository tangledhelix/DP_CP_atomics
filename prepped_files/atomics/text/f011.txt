
Introduction

With the dropping of the bomb on Hiroshima, the Atomic Age burst upon
us all, and with it came a frantic effort on the part of scientific people, newspapers,
magazines, and the radio to explain what it all meant. The words
atoms, atomic energy, atomic force, isotopes have been tossed about in the
public mind for many months, yet there is no real understanding of their
meaning. A thorough understanding would require that each person who
wishes to consider this important subject become a chemist or a physicist.
Obviously, there is not time for everyone in the United States to do this, and
in fact, such action might be highly undesirable. At the same time, some
knowledge of the subject is necessary if we are to understand the new age in
which we live.

In this book Dr. Eidinoff and Mr. Ruchlis have attempted to give a semi-popular
account of what these words mean and have discussed some of the
fundamental experiments upon which an understanding of the subject is
based. This seems to be the best way for the intelligent person to become
acquainted with the impact of science upon our modern civilization.

That these developments will have a far-reaching effect upon every phase
of our thinking is not to be doubted. A century ago the theory of evolution
arose, which has modified our modes of thought in many ways since then.
During this century the physical sciences are having a profound effect upon
our economic, social, and intellectual life. They also are making possible new
sources of energy which may either be useful or very destructive indeed. The
atomic bomb and beneficial applications of atomic energy will surely produce
as great a revolution in our political thinking as any important scientific
or industrial development of the past has ever done. Hence it is necessary
that we acquire an understanding of this subject; since the threat of destruction
by the atomic bomb hangs over us, it is necessary that we do this
as quickly as possible.

influence today is far greater than it has ever been in any period of the past,

broadest sense, in modern life. We also must understand how science works
