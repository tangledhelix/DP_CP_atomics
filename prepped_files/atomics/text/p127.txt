by many explorers in the field of atomic physics. Numerous transmutations
had already been produced in the laboratory in 1932 and 1933 by bombarding
atoms with high-speed particles. It seemed reasonable that at some point along
the way newly produced atoms, not found in nature, would be unstable and
emit radiations.

Historic discovery in Trance . . .

In 1934, this expectation was realized as a result of a most important discovery
in the field of atomic exploration by M. and Mme Joliot in France.
These brilliant investigators had previously
been very close to two other epochal finds
the neutron and the positron. Credit for
the discovery of the latter particles went,
however, to Chadwick of Great Britain and
Anderson of the United States. This time,
the glory of discovering artificial radioactivity
was well earned by the French team,
who received the Nobel prize in chemistry
in 1934.

The Joliots were bombarding an aluminum
target with alpha particles from a
polonium source. (Polonium was isolated
Marie Curie, the mother of Mme
Joliot.) As in the case of the bombardment
of other light elements such as beryllium
with alpha particles, neutrons were found
emanating from the aluminum during the
bombardment. However, a surprising observation
was made when the aluminum
target was placed in front of a Geiger
counter after the alpha bombardment had
stopped. Radiations were still being given
off by the target. The intensity of these radiations
became smaller as time elapsed.

radiations from naturally radioactive substances. The intensity died off fairly
