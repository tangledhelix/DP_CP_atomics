Now the key to the chain reaction and the continuing release of atomic
energy lies in the number of neutrons produced that are effective in bom-
barding new atoms of U235. If 101 of the 300 neutrons produced by the
previous collision strike new U235 atoms and cause them to "fiss," we can
have a successful chain reaction. Starting with 100 "fissing" uranium atoms,
we are now dealing with 101 after one round. These 101 atomic explosions
will in turn produce a new crop of neutrons which can again split a number
of new U235 atoms. Each time the number of split atoms increases slightly,
since the critical factor k is 1.01 (101 are produced from 100).

New links in the chain are forged very rapidly . . .

Even when the k number is allowed to remain as small as 1.01, the number
of atoms taking part in the chain soon grows to fantastic proportions.
Just as in the case of $100 increasing each year at an interest rate of 1 per
cent (k = 1.01), it would take 70 successive rounds of atomic explosions to
double the number of uranium atoms that are splitting. In another 70
rounds they would double again to 400. You can recognize now all the characteristics
of the enormously mounting growth in our previous illustrations.

The key to the rapidity of the growth is the time interval between one
group of atomic explosions and the succeeding group. It all depends upon
how long it takes for a neutron to split a U235 atom and create new ones.
But a neutron on the average moves at the rate of many miles in a second,
while the distance from one atom to the next is only about one one-hundred-millionth
of an inch! Thus the time factor must be measured in millionths
of a second. If we assume that the time it takes for the neutron to leave
a splitting U235 atom and smash into another one is about one-millionth of
a second, then the situation outlined above (with 100 neutrons creating
101) will explode the uranium in a fraction of a second. After 1,000 successive
chain explosions of atoms, the original 100 atoms shooting out neutrons
will have grown to more than 2 million atoms. In the next one-thousandth
of a second the 2 million atoms will have multiplied themselves
to more than 40 billion splitting atoms. In the next one-thousandth of a second
these will be increased to about 900 trillion atoms "fissing." In the next one-thousandth
