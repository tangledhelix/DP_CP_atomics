For this reason, the opening of the "atomic grocery store" at Oak Ridge,
Tenn., by the Manhattan Project about the middle of 1946 almost represents
a dream come true. Here is stock-piled a great assortment of useful radioactive
atoms, processed and laboriously purified by chemists and physicists
who are skilled in working near a pile and with highly radioactive sources.
Here about 50 different kinds of radioactive atoms are offered to qualified
people throughout the entire country, at a relatively nominal price. In a
grocery store we would buy tomatoes by the pound. In this atomic grocery
store we order material in an equally reasonable unit--the radioactive strength
of the purchase. A convenient unit for tracer work is the millicurie. Any
sample in which 37 million atoms are breaking up each second has a strength
of 1 millicurie. A single millicurie of many of these useful atoms will usually
be enough for hundreds of chemical experiments.

What price radioactivity? . . .

When we describe any interesting store, we should mention some of the
things sold in it and their price. Suppose we consider just a few of the important
isotopes.

We have already mentioned carbon 14. This atom has a half-life of approximately
5,000 years. This is favorable because the activity of a sample
does not diminish during any experiment. Before carbon 14 was discovered,
investigators had to use carbon 11, which was made using the cyclotron beam.
Unfortunately, carbon 11 decays to one-half its original intensity in about
21 minutes. Thus the short-lived carbon 11 was not suitable for long-term
experiments. In August, 1946, the cost of a millicurie of carbon 14 was about
$400. In April, 1947, the cost was reduced to $50.

A much less expensive but very useful isotope is radioactive phosphorus of
atomic weight 32. In 1947 the price was only $1 for a millicurie. Phosphorus
32 has a much shorter life than carbon 14 and decays to half its original activity
in about 14 days. It breaks down by ejecting a high-speed electron with
an energy of about 1.7 M.e.v. Great things are expected from the use of this
isotope not only in the study of the functioning of the organs of our bodies
but also as a means of arresting or possibly curing certain diseases. This use
will be described in the next chapter.
