
Atomic-energy rockets? . . .

The subject of space ships traveling almost effortlessly between the planets
has been a frequent companion of futuristic stories and comic-strip thrillers.
If, with atomic engines, speeds of 2,000 miles per hour could be attained, then
no place on this earth would be more than 6 hours away. What designer of a
suitable space ship will be the first to visit the moon and report back on his
experiences? What bold Columbus will venture to visit our planetary neighbor
Mars and at last bring back a decisive answer to that long-debated question
as to the existence of life on that planet?

Lest our imaginations run away with us, let us bear in mind that there are
many difficult problems to be solved before such a rocket ship can be produced.
The rocket ship propels itself forward by shooting out material in the
opposite direction. There is no known way of propelling a rocket out in space
other than by the actual loss of some material from the rocket itself. We can
if we wish hurl stones out the back end to send the ship forward. The greater
the speed with which the stone is hurled out backward, the greater the push
forward on the ship. But every time that stone is thrown out, it is gone forever
from the rocket. Even if we tried to rig up some device to capture and use
the stone over again in that same ship, we would find that the force required
to stop it and bring it back would exactly cancel out the forward kick and
we would have completely defeated our purpose.

Thus, a rocket must irretrievably lose some material that it carries along at
the start, regardless of whether it is powered by fuel or by atomic energy.

How then can atomic energy help? The key to the solution of this problem
lies in the velocity with which the material is shot out. Present-day rockets
using chemical fuels send their gases hurling out the back with speeds of
thousands of miles per hour to produce sufficient force to propel the ship.
If the speed of the gases coming out could be multiplied, then the speed of
the rocket ship could also be multiplied--using the same quantity of material.
If an atomic-energy motor could thus be built to cause the ejection of a gas
or other material at speeds of many miles per second, then it might become
practical to operate a space ship. The high speed of the propelling gases would
reduce the weight of material that must be carried.
