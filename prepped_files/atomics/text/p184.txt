And if we wave our hands about, we feel no substance around us. Yet if we
were magically endowed with the ability to see particles of molecular dimensions,
we would never forget the amazing spectacle that would greet our
eyes. We would see an enormous number of molecules of oxygen and of nitrogen,
with occasional molecules of other gases. A glassful of air would contain
about 7,000 million million million of these molecules. They are so small
that 100 million of them would have to be placed side by side in order to
fill up 1 inch along a ruler. We would be impressed by the speed with which
they are moving about in all directions. The oxygen molecules are speeding
along at the rate of over 1,000 miles per hour, about 1/4 mile per second!
If we focus our gaze on any particular molecule, we find that it does not
leave the vicinity at anywhere near that rate. For the molecules are so numerous
that they are continually colliding with each other and being deflected
from a straight path. The molecules in a teaspoonjul of gas suffer over 10,000
trillion trillion collisions with each other in 1 second. In fact, a molecule travels
on the average only about one one-hundred-thousandth of an inch before it
collides with a neighbor.

Gases tend to diffuse . . .

JUNIOR FORGOT THAT GASES DIFFUSE

We are all very familiar with the tendency of gases to diffuse and permeate
the air around them. When Junior pours out some hydrogen sulfide solution
in his cellar laboratory, the odor of rotten eggs soon arouses angry comments
from almost every part of the
house. The gas diffuses (spreads
out) until all the air is contaminated,
even in the absence of a
draft. If some greenish-yellow chlorine
gas is initially at the bottom
of a glass cylinder, it will gradually
diffuse upward until the color
in the entire cylinder is uniformly
green. This diffusion into the air
above takes place even though
chlorine is more than twice as
dense as air. It occurs because of
the very rapid motion of the individual molecules, which tend to spread out
until the gas completely fills the container into which it has been placed. If
there are no containing walls, the molecules of the gas will simply move out
into the air and mix intimately with the molecules of oxygen and nitrogen
already present in the atmosphere.
