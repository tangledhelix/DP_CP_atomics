
Finally, what evidence was there that radioactive phosphorus had been
formed? This was based on a very ingenious type of chemical evidence. This
technique is possible only because the Geiger counter is so sensitive that individual
high-speed charged particles such as positrons can be easily detected
with it. The aluminum foil was quickly dissolved in hydrochloric acid. The
products are aluminum chloride (a salt) and hydrogen gas. When the water
is evaporated from this solution, the solid salt remains. This salt contains
all the aluminum that was present in the foil. The salt was not radioactive,
proving that it was not the aluminum that was radioactive. But the gas that
came off during the addition of hydrochloric acid and subsequent evaporation
gave the same positron emission. That is just the result to be expected if radioactive
phosphorus were present. Minute quantities of phosphorus reacted
chemically with the hydrogen liberated from the hydrochloric acid to produce
the gas phosphine (formula PH3). This gas containing the radioactive
phosphorus was therefore also radioactive and emitted positrons.

The Joliots soon found that other targets could be used in producing man-made
(artificial) radioactivity. When alpha particles bombarded a boron
target, artificially radioactive nitrogen, having a half-life of 11 minutes, was
produced. In another experiment they bombarded a target of magnesium with
alpha particles and found that radioactive silicon atoms were formed. Half
of these newly formed atoms broke down to form daughter atoms in about 7
minutes.

The discoveries mentioned above represented only the very beginning of artificial
radioactivity. Three elements, phosphorus, nitrogen, and silicon, were
prepared in a radioactive form in the laboratory for the first time. By 1937,
close to 300, and by 1940, close to 400 had been discovered! The latter figure will
probably appear small within a few decades.

Artificial radioactivity by other nuclear collisions . . .

As we have seen, the first artificially radioactive isotopes were made
using alpha particles as the atomic projectiles. The yield of radioactive atoms
is not very high because the doubly charged alpha particles are subject to great
repulsive forces near the target nucleus and many of them are repelled from
the collision scene.
