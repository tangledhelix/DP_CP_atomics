The atomic bomb should go off even more rapidly . . .

Suppose we bring the pieces of the bomb together slowly. Then, as they
almost touch each other, the chain reaction will start. The energy set free will
no doubt act to separate the pieces again and the bomb will be a relatively harmless
dud. In other words, the pieces must be brought together very rapidly.
How rapidly? To help us answer this question, let us consider the chain reaction
itself.

We have seen how the ordinary uranium-carbon pile uses slow neutrons for
the controlled liberation of atomic energy. What would happen if the automatic
controls failed and the k value for the pile became greater than 1? The
pile would begin to liberate tremendous amounts of energy in a short time.
But since slow neutrons are carrying the chain, the material in the pile would
have a chance to expand and brcak apart almost as soon as the pile got out of
control. Only a minor explosion would result! Hence, a few elephants dropped
on an enemy city would have an effect about equivalent to dropping a pile
of ordinary uranium and carbon.

Use fast neutrons in bomb . . .

We now see why the material of the atomic bomb cannot be simply ordinary
uranium and carbon for slowing down the neutrons. Slow neutrons move at
speeds of only a mile or so a second. It is necessary to get rapid growth of
the chain by using fast neutrons moving at speeds of thousands of miles a
second. To get a chain reaction with fast neutrons, uranium 235 or plutonium
can be used. In this case, the enormous energy liberation--the equivalent of
thousands of tons of TNT--takes place so rapidly that the material of the
bomb hardly has a chance to expand during the fraction of a millionth of a
second in which most of the energy is probably liberated. The chain reaction
can thus proceed to liberate large amounts of energy before the bomb is blown
apart. The catastrophic results of such an explosion are known in general to
all of us and some of the details will shortly be described.
