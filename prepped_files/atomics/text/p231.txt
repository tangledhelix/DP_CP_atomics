Radiation from an operating pile . . .

We have already noted that as an atomic power pile operates, high-energy
neutrons, electrons, and gamma or x-radiations are produced. All these are
extremely hazardous to any personnel in the vicinity. The great care with
which radium is handled in hospitals and laboratories is familiar to many of
us. Few have failed to observe the elaborate precautions taken when x-rays are
used for therapy. Yet the amounts of radium used in hospitals are measured
in ten-thousandths of an ounce. It has been estimated that the amounts of
radioactive material produced by uranium fission in even a small pile may be
equivalent to many pounds of radium.

It was estimated by the scientists on the project that the fission products
arising from one day's operation of a 100,000-kilowatt pile could render
a large area uninhabitable if they were accidentally sprayed as a result of pile
explosion. It was shown in an earlier chapter that the operation of a cyclotron
does not come anywhere near producing practical amounts of atomic energy.
Yet recommendations for shielding a 60-inch cyclotron include: completely
surrounding walls of water at least 5 feet thick, a roof and floor equivalent
to 3 feet of water, a concrete walled room about 12 yards away.

Neutrons have a tendency to leak out through tiny holes and cracks in
about the same way that a gas will diffuse through tiny openings. Shields must
also be airtight because air exposed to the pile radiation becomes radioactive.

Replacing blind fear by knowledge . . .

It is well to remember, however, that the radiation hazard in atomic-power
operations is not a mysterious unknown factor that will dissuade scientists
from continuing their researches. In general, the best antidote to fear is
knowledge, and this knowledge has been carefully cultivated. A health
division was established at an early stage in the atomic-bomb projects whose
object was to ensure that no one suffered serious injury from the peculiar
hazards of atomic-pile work. The success of this group and of the general
precautions that were taken is attested by Smyth's statement that "factors of
safety used in plant design and operation are so great that the hazards of the
home and the family car are far greater for the personnel than any arising
from the plants."
