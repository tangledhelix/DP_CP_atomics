The bomb must be at least a certain size . . .

The critical size of an atomic bomb offers an interesting comparison with
an ordinary chemical bomb. Unfortunate laboratory accidents have taught us
that even small quantities of certain chemicals, such as nitroglycerin and mercury
fulminate, are capable of detonating violently. But there is a critical
size to an atomic bomb below which it cannot explode. A mass of plutonium
or U235 smaller than the critical size simply cannot explode because a chain
reaction is impossible. Too many neutrons would escape from the small mass
of material and break the chain. Furthermore, the ordinary methods of detonation
do not apply in the case of an atomic bomb. Once the size has exceeded
the critical point, the chain reaction occurs at an incredibly rapid rate. Even
one stray neutron (and there are many present in the atmosphere at all times
from cosmic-ray bombardments, etc.) can start the chain reaction. For this
reason, we may say that the bomb reaches its critical size at the last instant of
its existence.

Simply bring the separate parts of the bomb together . . .

In principle, setting off an atomic bomb should not be too difficult. Estimates
of the size of the fissionable material needed in a bomb range from
2 to 200 pounds--at most, the size of a basketball. Just for the sake of illustration,
let us say that the bomb must contain 100 pounds of atomic explosive--U235
or plutonium. This 100-pound figure then represents the critical mass,
the smallest amount of material in which a chain reaction can develop, in
which atomic energy can be liberated. Now make two lumps of this material,
each weighing 60 pounds and separated from each other. Nothing happens,
because each lump is below the critical size. Now bring the two lumps together.
The size is above the critical, and even one stray neutron coming in from
the atmosphere is enough to start the chain reaction going. In other words,
setting off the bomb simply means bringing the pieces together. "Well, if this
is so simple," the reader will ask, "why was so much effort put into the manufacture
of the bomb itself? It appears to be child's play!" In principle, yes.
