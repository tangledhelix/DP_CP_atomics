No longer an academic problem . . .

When the extremely important fact was learned early in 1940 that the
lighter isotope of uranium was responsible for the majority of the fissions in
a uranium sample, it became a matter of more than academic interest to attempt
its large-scale separation. But the task of separating large quantities
of this isotope appeared formidable indeed. In the first place, 1,000 atoms of
natural uranium contain only 7 U235 atoms and 993 U238 atoms. (We shall
here neglect the small number of atoms having an atomic weight of 234.)

The construction and successful operation of an enormous plant using this
process represents still another "impossible" feat that was actually achieved.
Research was started at Columbia University in 1940 under the general leadership
of Harold C. Urcy and John R. Dunning. The Kellex Corporation (a
unit of The M. W. Kellogg Co.) designed the full-scale plant which was to be
turned over to the Carbide & Carbon Chemicals Corp. for operation. A review
of some of the accomplishments and difficulties of this process high-lights
the enormity of the task that was undertaken.

Finding a suitable gas containing uranium . . .

It is a familiar fact that compounds containing metal atoms are usually
solids at room temperature and up to several hundred degrees Fahrenheit. It
would be very difficult to find a compound containing gold, for instance, that
is gaseous at practical operating temperatures. A compound containing uranium
and fluorine, called uranium hexafluoride, has been known to chemists
for many years. A molecule of this compound contains one uranium atom and
six fluorine atoms in chemical combination. Although a solid at room temperature,
it has the unusual property of going over to the gaseous condition under
ordinary pressure at a temperature of only 1330 F, (about the temperature of
warm water). This gas was therefore considered a possible "process gas."
Since it is highly reactive chemically, the search was continued for more suitable
gases.
