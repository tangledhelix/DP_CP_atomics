Up to that time two theories of light, the wave theory of Huygens and the
corpuscular, or particle, theory of Newton, had vied with each other for acceptance
over more than a century and a half. Both these theories have been
important in the development of modern theories of light.

According to Huygens, light was transmitted as a wave motion. Like a
water wave it radiated out from the source in ever-widening circles. Light
waves could differ in length from crest to crest in the same manner that a
ripple differs from the waves caused by a large boat. This distance from crest
to crest is known as the wavelength.

The wave could also be described from its frequency. Thus, if you noticed
five waves pass by every minute, the frequency of the wave would be 5 per
minute. Another wave which had a frequency of 20 per minute would consist
of a greater number of waves spaced more closely. The wavelength of
this higher frequency wave would thus be shorter.

Using this wave theory of Huygens, we are able to explain many of the
observed facts about light. Different colors are ascribed to differences in frequency.
A red wave differs in frequency from a green wave or a violet wave.
These frequencies arc enormous in the case of visible light. Were it possible
for you to stand at a given spot with some special stop watch and count the
number of waves of violet light that go by in 1 second, you would get the
astounding number of 1 million billion per second. The length of the wave
would be about 1/80,000 inch. A red wave would be of lower frequency
half as many waves per second as for violet.

Sir Isaac Newton, on the other hand, developed a corpuscular theory which
stated that light consisted of high-speed particles shooting out of the luminous
object. According to this theory, different colors were simply due to different
particles in white light.
