Another method of treatment of specific cancers may be the use of high-energy
proton bullets produced by a cyclotron. These might be used in a carefully
controlled manner for deep-seated cancers. These protons have smaller
destructive power at the start of their journey through the body than near
the end of their range. Thus, by controlling the energy of the particle produced,
it may be possible to shoot high-energy protons through the outer
layers of flesh so as to create their greatest destruction at a predetermined
depth, where they will have maximum effect on the deeply embedded cancerous
tissue.

Tragedy accompanied the first use of x-rays . . .

We have already seen how a fascinating branch of science was born when
Wilhelm Roentgen observed in 1895 that "mysterious" radiations from the
discharge of a cathode-ray tube could penetrate considerable thicknesses of
matter. Since their nature and origin were unknown, the radiations were
called x-rays. With some improvements in the original design, it was found
that these rays could make the bones of the hand and other internal parts
of the body visible to the eye. Thus the first applications made by physicians
involved the diagnosis and treatment of bone injuries and fractures. Since
the general public was at first a bit loath to undergo examination by the
awesome x-ray machine, early "radiologists" would repeatedly expose their
own hands in demonstrating its use. Furthermore, no effort was made to localize
or focus the x-ray emission. As a result, these investigators were continually
exposed to the radiations. Few ill effects were observed at first but the insidious
action of the rays was cumulative. Malignant growths and severe, deep tissue
diseases began to appear in the hands, arms, shoulders, and other parts of
their bodies. The story of the subsequent lives of many of the pioneer radiologists
is indeed a sad tale. In order to check the growth of the malignant tissue,
frequent amputations became necessary. Surely, these early pioneers may be
termed true martyrs of science!

It was in this unfortunate manner that the destructive effect of these x-rays
on tissue was first discovered. The question then arose, Might not this radiation
be applied to the destruction of cancerous tissue as well? A vigorous
attack was made on the problem and considerable success achieved. By careful
experimentation it became possible to standardize doses of radiation and
obtain accurate control.
