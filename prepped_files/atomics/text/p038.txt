
According to Thomson, the other atoms are also made up of these fundamental
packages of electricity, the electron and the proton. Even though this
is still a hazy picture, it is a step forward. Soon other clever observations
were to give us a deeper insight into the architecture of all atoms.

Electrons and protons explain many things . . .

With this bold venture by Thomson, the jigsaw puzzle of the structure of
all materials began to fit together and the explanation of many observations
became clearer.

What does it mean to electrify an object by rubbing it with another, an observation
first made thousands of years ago? Normally, matter consists of equal
amounts of positive and negative electricity in the form of protons and electrons.
Rub two pieces of matter together and some of the more easily moved
electrons in one object transfer to the other. Therefore, the object which gains
electrons becomes negative, while the other which has lost electrons becomes
positive to the same degree.

Where do the cathode rays come from? The metal making up the negative
terminal consists of electrons and positive particles. Apparently, some electrons
are less firmly attached to this metal terminal and are induced by the positive
charge on the opposite terminal to cross over the "empty tube." These electrons
exist in the metal of the cathode. The cathode was made negative by pushing
into the metal an excess number of electrons, which repel each other because
they are like charges. Consequently, they distribute themselves around the
outside of the metal atoms and on the outside of the electrode. A positive charge
(made by removing electrons) exists on the positive terminal. When the attraction
becomes great enough the electrons can jump the gap. The resulting
electron streams are the cathode rays.

Where do the positive rays come from? Mainly from the molecules of gas
that are still present in the tube. The high-speed electrons leaving the cathode
can smash into these molecules, rip off electrons from them, and sometimes
separate the atoms that paired up to make the molecules. We now have positive
ions and more electrons. The positive ions are attracted to the negative electrode
and many of them pass through the holes in this terminal to form the
positive rays.

Can anyone appreciate the size of these numbers? . . .

Finding out the charge on the electron and the actual weight of atoms using
Thomson's method has given us a reliable value for the enormous number of
molecules and atoms present in a sample of matter.
