Water is not the only liquid that can be used for circulation in the pile.
The liquids chosen must meet several important requirements. Efficient
steam-engine operation requires high temperatures. Consequently, the liquid
must remain stable at high temperatures. It must not cause corrosion of the
materials in the pile. And it must not capture an excessive number of neutrons
and stop the chain. It is to be expected that materials other than water will be
used. In fact, certain molten metals look promising in this respect and may
replace water as the circulating fluid.

Calling in the plumber will be of little use . . .

It is important for us to keep in mind that installations of this kind must
be practically foolproof, for the intense radiations of the pile make the component
parts so radioactive that adjustments near the pile become very difficult.
In current large-scale engineering operations, occasional shutdowns are
necessitated by leaks at pipe joints, cracks in piping, and the effects of
specific corrosion. In such cases the welder, plumber, and pipe fitter can approach
the troublesome spot and repair the cause of the trouble. Not so with
many pile operations. This maintenance factor is probably the most challenging
to our leading engineers. In addition, all the uses of atomic energy must
be free of any dangers to the consumer in the form of radiations carried by the
air, water, or steam coming from the pile.

Look for large installations . . .

The common heat producers, coal, oil, and gasoline, and their engines,
steam engines, diesel and gasoline engines, have few limitations as to size.
There are tiny model-plane gasoline engines and giant 3,000-horsepower airplane
engines. There are tiny palm-sized steam engines and giant steamship
engines. These various sizes can be adapted to a variety of purposes and uses.
Not so with existing atomic-energy engines.
