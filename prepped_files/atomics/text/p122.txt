As far back as 1929, Millikan and his coworkers at the California Institute
of Technology had begun intensive work in order to be able to measure
cosmic-ray energies amounting to approximately 1 billion electron volts. Their
method of attack on this problem was bold yet straightforward. High-energy
electrified particles streaking through a cloud chamber usually show up as
straight tracks in a photograph. We have explained earlier how a moving
charge is deflected by a magnetic field applied to the particle. As a result, the
particle is forced to move in a circular path. If the particle drifts into a magnetic
field at a slow speed, then the magnetic field will have more time to act and
will force the particle to move in a relatively small circle. If the particle speeds
into the magnetic field with great velocity, there will be less time for it to
curve. An extremely high speed particle will have an almost straight track.
Thus the amount of curving of the track is a measure of the energy and speed
of the particle.

Obviously, in order to deflect a particle of tremendous energy, such as a billion
electron volts, it is necessary to use a very powerful magnetic field. At
the California Institute of Technology, a cloud chamber had been placed in
an upright position and surrounded by a powerful electromagnet. The instrument
was fitted with a camera so that the tracks of particles deflected
magnetic fields could be photographed and studied.

On an afternoon in August, 1932, Dr. Carl D. Anderson took a photograph
of one of these tracks and developed the film. A diagram showing the appearance
of the track is reproduced here. Offhand, it might appear to be similar
to many others found in this book. But close study by Anderson revealed a
highly unusual feature and led to an extremely unorthodox conclusion. There
are few examples in modern science that so well illustrate the delights of
scientific and unprejudiced reasoning, or more popularly, scientific sleuthing.
Let us attempt to reconstruct some of this train of thought.

it. The simplest starting assumption that we may make is that a single particle
entered the lead plate on one side and came out the other side. This is highly
probable, because the over-all path appears so continuous.
