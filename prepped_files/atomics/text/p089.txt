
Chapter Nine

Modern Alchemy

Alchemy once a thriving fraud . . .

Earlier, in the course of reviewing atomic adventures, we spoke of the
vain search of the alchemists of the Middle Ages for ways of transmuting
the baser metals, such as lead, into gold. Some of the alchemists
made honest efforts to extract this secret from nature. Many others were frauds
and practiced their quackery as a means of livelihood. Their followers were
not simply among the uneducated of that period; alchemists were found in
the courts of kings. So gullible were some rulers that it seemed to them an
easy task to replenish their supply of gold by simply making it from cheap
materials. In 1445, King Henry VI of England became interested in such
a venture and many a worthless coin, passed off as gold, was given out to the
people. A scientist of such great esteem as Sir Isaac Newton considered that
transmutation of elements in the chemical laboratory was possible. This was
almost 1700. Even during the nineteenth century fraudulent corporations were
organized with the apparent purpose of making gold from cheaper metals.
Around 1900 a syndicate was organized in New York City for this purpose
but was disbanded after enough capital had been plucked from a gullible
public.
