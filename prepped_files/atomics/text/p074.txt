Other facts of a similar nature began to appear. The element thorium (for
which use had been found in old-fashioned gas mantles) is the radioactive
parent atom of another family, the final offspring of which is also lead--but
this time with an atomic weight of 208! Certainly, one pillar of Dalton's early
atomic theory was beginning to crumble. Atoms of the same element need
not have the same atomic weight. The essential point, of course, is that they
must have the same atomic number, or charge on the nucleus.

was now necessary to give a name to atoms that had the same atomic
number but different atomic weight. They might be called chemical twins
because, except for their weight, their chemical properties are so nearly alike.
Chemists refer to such atom pairs as isotopes. Their discovery has had a profound
influence not only on the course of chemistry and physics in the past 30
years, but on the development of atomic energy.

Building models of the nucleus . . .

Early in this chapter, the problem of setting up the architecture of the atom
occupied our attention. As soon as the work of Moseley and Rutherford
had established the meaning of the atomic number, the curiosity of scientists
turned toward the structure of the nucleus. Since protons and electrons were
the recognized fundamental particles at that time, models were naturally
built on this basis.

Building these nuclei or cores of atoms is an interesting game. In the years
1913 to about 1932 these were the rules: In the nucleus are protons and electrons.
The protons have a weight of almost 1 on the chemists' scale and account
for practically all the weight of the nucleus. Let us try this game for the
first two elements.

Hydrogen has an atomic number of 1 and a weight of 1. This is simple. The
nucleus is simply a proton. Now take helium, atomic number 2, atomic weight
4. Suppose we place 4 protons and 2 electrons together and call this the nucleus.
The weight checks--4 protons have a weight of 4. The charge checks--4
positive charges and 2 negative charges correspond to a net charge of +2.
Indeed, your authors were first taught to play the game in this manner. But
since the early thirties, students of the atom nucleus have become fairly
certain that equally simple but different rules must be used.
