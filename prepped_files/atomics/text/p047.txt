
In 1905 Einstein pointed out that radiant energy may be considered to
exist in the form of these "energy packages"--an idea similar to the corpuscles
of Newton. In this energy package the higher frequency possessed the
greater energy.

The photon--a package of radiant energy . . .

The revolutionary idea of a package of radiant energy, today called the
photon, received rapid confirmation from a number of separate sources.
It had been observed that light has the ability to eject electric particles from
certain materials. Today we know
that these particles are the electrons.
A peculiar fact was observed
about this photoelectric effect. The
higher the frequency of light, the
greater its effectiveness in ejecting
electrons from metals. In fact,
where red light was absolutely incapable
of ejecting electrons from a
certain metal, violet light might be
found effective. Where violet light
failed, ultraviolet light might serve
for that purpose. And x-rays, of
higher frequency than ultraviolet, were far more effective in ejecting electrons
from materials.

CONTINUOUS WAVES?

OR PHOTONS?

The idea of an energy package, or photon, was applied to these observations.
The photon "bullets" of higher frequency radiations such as x-rays
have more energy than the photons of lower frequency radiations. Hence when
an x-ray photon strikes an object, electrons are ripped away from atoms in the
material. These electrons are ejected at high speeds and in turn can break up
molecules in their path into charged fragments, or ions.

Red light, with low frequency and low energy, would be less effective in
ejecting electrons than green, violet, or ultraviolet. The electron in each atom
required a definite "push" to tear it away from the attraction of the positive
portion. If the red light did not have enough energy to supply the proper
push, then no matter how intense a beam of red light we used, no electrons
would ever be kicked out. A higher energy blue light photon might have the
needed amount of energy and could thus start the ejection of electrons, no
matter how faint a beam of light it was.
