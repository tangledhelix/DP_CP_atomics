In 1886, the German investigator Goldstein was working with a cathode-ray
tube, the negative terminal of which was perforated with a number of holes.
He noticed streams of luminous gas behind the negative terminal. Moving in
a direction opposite to the cathode rays, they seemed to pass down the tube,
through the holes of the cathode until they were stopped by the glass. What
were they?

About 1907, J. J. Thomson attempted to discover the nature of these positive
particles in the same way that he had determined the nature of the electron.
After the positive rays passed through the holes of the negative terminal of the
tube, he caused them to swerve by applying electric and magnetic forces. He
allowed the deflected rays to strike a photographic film, which became exposed
wherever the rays struck. In this way, he made many photographs of
the positive-ray tracks, with a variety of different gases in the tube. He was
surprised by the difference between the behavior of the cathode rays and the
positive rays. The cathode rays all deflected in the same manner. But the
positive rays could be deflected in groups, each group causing a separate track
on the photographic film, just as though there were groups of different particles
in the rays.

the gas that was used. From these observations Thomson attempted to find
out what electric charges were carried by the positive rays. It soon became clear
