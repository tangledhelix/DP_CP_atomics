During these thousands of years while the horse was our foremost source
of power, a gradual improvement in simple machines and tools took place.
The pulley, the wheel and axle, the inclined plane, the lever had been discovered.
These were modified to form the wedge for cutting and splitting, the
screw for fastening and tightening, the gear and the wheel and chain for
altering speed. Not one of these mechanical gadgets provided any energy
for causing motion. They merely made it easier to do the required job. A man
or a horse was still required to push or pull these devices in order to achieve
the desired result.

Note that three separate steps are needed in order to enable us to get work
done. First we need some source of energy in order to create motions, apply
forces, and thereby do work. Then we need some engine or motor which will
convert this energy into motion. And finally we need a combination of simple
machines in order to utilize these motions and forces conveniently. A man
lifting weights is a motor or engine doing work. The energy supply for
this engine comes from the food he consumes. His muscles, his limbs, and his
joints are arranged as combinations of simple levers to utilize the motions produced
as effectively as possible.

Early robots . . .

The key to the industrial revolution of modern times was the development of
a lifeless metal engine and motor to replace man and his beasts of burden
as the prime movers. The enormous multiplication of power that has taken
place in the past two and a half centuries since the invention of the steam
engine has made possible the tremendous change from relatively primitive
farming life to the exceedingly complex and varied industrial civilization of
today.

It is interesting to note that the first working steam engine was invented
more than 2,000 years ago in Greece, It may be a surprise to learn that this
engine (invented by Heron to open temple doors) operated on a jet-propulsion
principle. But in the thousands of years since then only a few isolated uses
of motive powers other than those of man and the horse were in actual use.
Windmills and water wheels had limited use during this period for a few
specialized jobs. The energy of the wind in propelling sailing vessels was
the most important of these otherwise limited uses.
