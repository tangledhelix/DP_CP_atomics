Chapter Twelve

Man-made Radioactivity

Searching for man-made unstable atoms . . .

he story of the discovery of natural radioactivity has already been
related. We have described, as part of this story, the generation of
daughter elements and the emission of alpha particles, electrons, and
gamma rays. This natural radioactivity takes place mainly among the heavier
elements above lead, atomic number 82. There is also some very slight radioactivity
in a few of the lighter elements found in nature.

The changes involved in the natural transmutation of the elements are so
deep-seated and involve so much energy that man in the laboratory is unable
to alter or affect them using ordinary techniques. The rate of decay of a sample
of radium is not affected measurably by cooling it in liquid air or heating it
to the highest temperatures attainable in the laboratory.

Up to 1934, we had to use these naturally radioactive substances for our
specialized purposes, such as atomic artillery sources, and in the attack on
various diseases. But these natural sources are rare and expensive. An ounce
of radium in recent years might cost in the neighborhood of half a million
dollars!

prepare radioactive isotopes of the more common elements in the laboratory?
