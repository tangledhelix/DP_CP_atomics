
Looking back at Dalton's atomic theory . . .

We have already seen how Dalton's atomic theory paved the way toward
understanding the essential facts of chemistry. Even today the everyday experiences
of the average chemist are interpreted in the light of Dalton's
invisible atom. Few observations appeared in the interval from 1808 to the end
of that century to challenge it. But in the past 50 years, beginning with the
discovery of radium, a number of major corrections to Dalton's atomic theory
have been made.

We have found that atoms can be divided into smaller units, although with
much greater effort than is required to break up molecules. We have found
atoms of the same element having different weights, which we call isotopes.
We have found it possible to convert the atoms of one element into another.
And we have also found that it is not quite true that the weights of the
materials used originally and the weights of the products resulting from
them must be the same. Today we know that some of the weight of the
original materials can be destroyed and converted into enormous amounts of
energy, as occurs in the atomic bomb.

It is as though we were describing a brick as a fundamental unit in the building
of a wall. We might refer to it as an indivisible unit in terms of the construction
of the wall. But the brick itself is made of other things and can be
divided further if we wish to expend the energy required to do it. Dalton's
atomic theory of indivisible atoms served the nineteenth century well despite
its limitations today. It still serves the chemist well but is not adequate for
modern atomic sleuthing.
