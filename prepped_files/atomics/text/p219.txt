wires comes from steam turbines driven by the heat energy of coal, or from
water power supplied by giant dams.

With the electric energy thus produced it is possible to run motors at a
distance, light electric lamps, operate radios and electric heaters, and perform
a multitude of other tasks. The great utility of electric energy arises from the
ease with which it can be converted into any of the other energy forms and
the simple manner in which it can be "transported" over stationary wires.

The manifold machines and engines which mankind has at his disposal
have provided for his use an enormous supply of mechanical man power to
do his bidding. In the United States there are at least four mechanical "slaves,"
in the form of various motors and engines, for every man, woman, and child.

Atomic energy is no secret to the sun . . .

Coal remains today as a basic source of energy for our industrial civilization,
driving steam turbines that operate most of our electric generators. Prominent
in importance are oil and gasoline used in running automobiles, airplanes,
and many trains and boats. Of great importance today are our giant hydroelectric
plants with their energy derived from the force of falling water trapped
by enormous dams.

In all these cases the original source of the energy is the atomic energy of
the sun. In its interior--at temperatures of millions of degrees--it is believed
that hydrogen nuclei are gradually combining through a complex series of
collisions involving carbon and nitrogen atoms to form helium. When four
hydrogen atoms become transmuted to one helium atom, approximately 1
per cent of their original mass is annihilated. This lost mass appears as the
sun's radiant energy. There is much evidence to show that this gigantic alchemy
keeps the i-million-mile ball of hot gas, our sun, at a surface temperature
of about 10,000° F and an interior temperature estimated at millions of
degrees. Approximately 4 million tons of mass arc annihilated each second
in the sun to supply the gigantic splashes of energy that keep us alive.

This energy radiates from the sun in the form of light, including the infrared
and ultraviolet. Our tiny globe--93 million miles away from the sun--receives
only about a two-billionth of the sun's energy. Yet this amounts to 1
horsepower for every square yard upon which the sunlight falls!
