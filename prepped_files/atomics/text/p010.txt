
Some time later, in 1766, eccentric Lord Henry Cavendish discovered hydrogen
gas, the lightest material in our present list of elements. He also observed
the simple but important fact that water was formed whenever hydrogen was
burned in air. In 1774 another brilliant Englishman, Joseph Priestley, discovered
oxygen, the familiar gas so essential to life, and found that "a candle
burned in this gas with a remarkably vigorous flame."

A short time later Antoine Lavoisier found that air seemed to be composed
of two gases. In one of these gases, a candle would burn brightly; in the other
the candle flame died out. The first gas was called oxygen, while the second
gas is today called nitrogen. These experiments removed some of the mysteries
concerning the nature of fire.

What does it mean to say that "something is burning"? Disproving a theory
that had prevailed for many years, Lavoisier showed that burning was simply
the chemical combination of a substance with oxygen in the air. From this it
followed that water could not be considered an element because it could be
made by burning hydrogen in air, that is, by combining hydrogen with oxygen.

A historic argument was finally settled . . .

With the help of reliable and delicate scales, it became possible to answer
a pressing question. Many of us are familiar with the fact that water is made
up of hydrogen and oxygen, that common table salt can be made from sodium
and chlorine, and that iron rust is formed from iron and oxygen. In what
proportions do the hydrogen and oxygen combine to form water? How many
pounds of hydrogen and how many of oxygen are present in 10 pounds of
water? Is there a definite answer or can the proportions vary for any one
compound, in the same manner that a chef can vary the ingredients in a
recipe?

Claude de Berthollet, friend of Lavoisier and adviser to Napoleon, took the
position that the proportions could vary, that salt from the Indian Ocean might
be made up differently than salt from the Gulf of Mexico.

On the other hand, Joseph Proust, another French chemist, attempted to
defend the position that there is a definite proportion in the case of any compound
such as salt, water, iron rust, etc. The heated argument began in 1799
and raged for 8 years. The two contestants tried more and more experiments,
carefully purifying all the materials that were used and then examining the
results. In the end, Proust was the winner. One could be confident that
