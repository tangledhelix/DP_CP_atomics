has passed. It is therefore possible with this apparatus to see and photograph,
just as with a tracer bullet or in sky-writing with airplanes, the exact path of
the high-speed particles shot out from the radium.

Other tools were used to "survey the wreckage" . . .

A number of tools were used to study the nature of the radiations. Almost
all these depend on the fact that the radiations ionize the air around them,
leaving in their wake a large number of electrically charged fragments of atoms
and molecules. The measuring instruments simply record the extent of ionization.

Among the simplest of these tools is a very useful instrument called the
electroscope, which found special use in the detection of the cosmic rays.
Another useful tool is the Geiger counter, which is used to count the number
of particles emitted by a radioactive material. Both these instruments will be
described in later chapters.

Using these tools, it was soon discovered that the radiations from radioactive
atoms are of three distinct varieties. These are referred to as alpha particles,
beta particles, and gamma rays. What is their nature?

Alpha particles are present in the radiations . . .

The cloud chamber, the Geiger counter, and the electroscope were used as
ace detectives in studying the nature of the radiations. The cloud chamber
was particularly useful in identifying the types of particles, their charges, and
their weights. Electric and magnetic forces can be applied in the chamber
space and deflections of individual particles photographed.

Some of the particles shot out from radium were attracted by negative plates
and were thus shown to be positive. These were called alpha particles. Using
Thomson's techniques of measuring speed, weight, and charge of moving
particles in magnetic and electric fields, it was possible to identify the alpha
particle. Its atomic weight proved to be 4, the same as that of helium. It appeared
