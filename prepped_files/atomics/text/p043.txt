Fingerprinting the elements . . .

At about this time a most important use for the spectrum was developed--one
that was to play an important part in the search for the secrets of the atom.
It had been noted that the breaking up of white light into its colors in the
spectrum could be used to analyze the colors of objects. Thus a purple light
passed through a prism would separate into red and blue, with dark
spaces for the other colors which do not exist to any extent in purple. It was
soon observed that when various elements were placed in flames they gave
off characteristic colors, which could then be analyzed by passing the color
through a glass prism. Thus, it was found that compounds containing sodium
always gave a yellow color in a flame. When analyzed with a prism, the light
from sodium appeared mainly as two distinct lines in the yellow portion of
the spectrum. Later, with the development of the Geissler tube, it was found
that when different gases or vapors were placed in the tube and an electric
spark sent through, each element gave a characteristic spectrum consisting of
bright lines of color.

atoms. If an unknown material is placed in a spark or in a Geissler tube, it

off by all other materials. Thus, neon gives colors that are predominantly red
as observed in neon signs. Mercury gives off mainly blue and violet colors that
