
Still another approach for the metallurgist and the chemist is the possibility
of an actual change in the properties of materials under neutron bombardment.
Thus, improvements in such properties of materials as hardness may
be achieved by treatment with neutrons given off from the pile. There are
great possibilities here that will occupy many investigators in the near future.

We know that the strength and other important properties of metals and
alloys are greatly influenced by the size and shape of the tiny crystals making
up bulk metal. Traces of impurities tend to settle out in the boundaries between
the tiny grains and thereby affect the properties of the alloy. A wide
variety of tagged metal atoms, made by using neutron beams from the pile,
can be employed in tracing the action of the metal atoms and their impurities.
Thus we can look for substantial contributions by tagged atoms to the work
of the metallurgist.

One important line of development for radioactive atoms may be their use
as substitutes for x-ray machines. The gamma rays from radioactive atoms
are in general more penetrating than x-rays. An x-ray photograph can therefore
be taken simply by placing an object between a photographic film and a
tiny bit of radioactive material and exposing the film for the proper time. The
simplicity of such an arrangement would make it far more convenient than
the bulky x-ray machines now used in industry. It may also be possible to
control the harmful effects of the gamma rays on live flesh so that x-ray pictures
of the human body using such radioactive sources may be made. One interesting
possibility is the use of such an "x-ray machine" inside the human
body to take a shadow picture from the inside out. Thus, a piece of radioactive
material placed inside a body cavity near a part to be examined might
make a more detailed shadow picture than is at present possible with existing
x-ray apparatus.

Other important contributions to chemistry are envisaged from the possible
production of new materials resulting from radiation bombardment. High-energy
particles have the ability to create new arrangements of molecules.
Thus bombardment of materials with radiations from a pile, or from radioactive
materials, may produce changes sought by the chemist. This may be
of value in the manufacture of new plastics and synthetic rubber, in oil refining,
and in the production of various drugs and vaccines.

One of the most exciting aspects of our acquisition of large-scale atomic
energy deals with new weapons with which we can hope to attack the problem
of disease. Here too, broad highways have suddenly been opened for us.
Despite the fact that we are only beginning to traverse these roads, let us
attempt to project some of the early landmarks and assess some of the potentialities
of the new atomic science in prolonging our lives.
