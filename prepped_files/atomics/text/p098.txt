Atoms and stars . . .

Some time betore Einstein's formula for matter-energy conversions was
presented to the world, a fundamental question concerning the energy supply
of the sun had puzzled scientists. The sun radiates enormous amounts of
energy, sufficient to keep our own and other planets well supplied with heat
and light. In fact, if we could capture all the energy of the sunlight falling on
i square yard of surface on the earth in i second and convert it completely
into mechanical energy, it would be equivalent to more than i horsepower.
When we consider that the earth has well over ioo million million square yards
of surface intercepting sunlight and that the distance from the sun to the earth
is 93 million miles, it is obvious that the sun must be radiating stupendous
amounts of energy. Only one two-billionth part of this energy is actually
captured by the earth. How explain this enormous energy supply of the
sun?

If the sun derived its energy from ordinary chemical processes such as the
burning of coal or oil, it would not last for more than several thousand years.
If that were the case, the sun would long ago have cooled off to a dead star,
leaving us to freeze to death in short order. Recorded history, evidence of tree
rings going back thousands of years, our own accurate astronomical observations,
all show that there has been no appreciable change in the radiation
power of the sun for many thousands of years. In fact, we estimate the age
of the earth and the sun in billions of years. Where, then, does the sun's supply
of energy come from? Einstein's energy-matter relationship gives us an answer.
In the million-degree temperatures inside the sun, matter is somehow
being destroyed at the rate of 4 million tons per second and is being converted
into enormous quantities of energy, splashed out into space. Although this
might seem a large amount of matter to lose, it is but a drop in the ocean to the
sun, which is almost a million miles in diameter and a million times larger
than the earth. At that rate of loss of matter, the sun has enough energy supply
for many billion years of additional life.

But now a number of pertinent questions might be asked. How do we
know that this relation stated by Einstein is correct? What direct evidence
have we obtained for it? Here our atomic billiards game has been of the
greatest aid. In this way the mysteries residing in enormous stars can be
penetrated by studying the atom.
