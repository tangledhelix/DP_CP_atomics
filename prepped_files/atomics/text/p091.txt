and perhaps millions, of times greater than could be then obtained
in the laboratory. This was demonstrated by the fact that the radiations could
penetrate various thicknesses of matter. Further, heating the radioactive
material to thousands of degrees in the laboratory could not effect the natural
radioactivity at all!

It became clear at once that the alchemists were not seeking an impossible
or ridiculous goal. They simply did not have the means at their disposal to

change one atom into another. If they could have produced temperatures
equal to millions of degrees (such as are present in the sun) or if they had
known how to speed up particles to millions of times the energies of ordinary
gas molecules, they would no doubt have succeeded in transmuting the
elements. However, with the means at the disposal of the present-day scientist,
this can be effected. This very fascinating field of work may properly be
called modern alchemy.

Modern alchemy and a game of billiards . . .

is a fact of considerable historical interest that the discovery of natural
radioactivity not only demonstrated why the alchemists of old were not successful
but also gave man the first tools with which to change one atom into
another successfully. The high-energy alpha particles from radioactive atoms
could now be used as high-speed bullets aimed to hit a variety of target atoms.

At this point an objection might be raised. The target atoms are only about
one one-hundred-millionth of an inch in diameter. The bullets are equally
small. Neither can be seen by the naked eye or by a microscope, and there is
very little probability that they ever will be seen. How then can this artillery
practice ever be successful? True enough, the chances of scoring a hit with
one bullet and one target atom are vanishingly small. But in actual practice
millions of bullets are shot out at trillions of target atoms. Only a few collisions
result, but these few can be studied by the use of the cloud chamber
and other devices, and a great deal can be learned about what actually takes
place. On this basis, the laboratory-controlled transmutation of the elements
was first achieved by Rutherford in 1919.

simple in plan, although the details are considerably more complex. The
method might be described as "atomic billiards," since it makes use of the

tracks of the cloud chamber. We treat the bullets shooting out of radium or
