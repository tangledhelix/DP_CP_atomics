
This hunt can be broken down, roughly, into two major periods. The
first, lasting up to the end of 1942, culminated in the actual construction
of a working atomic-energy machine, forerunner of future peacetime
atomic-energy machines. After that, the major problem revolved around
the production of bomb material and its manufacture into atomic bombs.
When the full import of atomic energy is realized in the distant future,
it may well be that historians will mark the beginning of the Atomic Age
not from 1945, when the first bomb was dropped, but from 1942, when the
first machine was actually operated.

The operation of this machine depends upon starting and controlling a
chain reaction in uranium. This success hinged upon a certain critical
factor, which was to act as the focus for all scientific research, culminating
in the creation of the first atomic-energy machine.

The critical factor "k"--success or failure . . .

The operation of an atomic-energy machine depends upon the successful
maintenance of a chain reaction in which one neutron splits an atom and
releases other neutrons, which in turn split other atoms. But there is no
guarantee that these secondary neutrons released by a fission starting with
one neutron will be effective in splitting other atoms.

Suppose that we start with a lump of uranium and shoot 100 neutrons
into it from an outside source. Some of these will strike U235 atoms, cause
fission, and thus bring about the creation of new neutrons. Others, however,
will be lost by various means. Some will simply escape from the
uranium into the air. Some will be captured by U238 atoms, by other
materials, by impurities, or by the walls of the container. However, if from
these 100 original neutrons, 105 new ones result after all these gains and
losses, then k is 105 divided by 100, or 1.05. We have multiplied the number
of neutrons by 1.05. This represents a growth of 5 per cent. As long as
k is even a little greater than 1, a chain reaction is possible. But if 99 new
neutrons are formed, then k is 99 divided by 100, or 0.99. As long as k is
even a tiny fraction less than 1, a chain reaction is impossible, and no large-scale
atomic energy would be produced by the pile.
