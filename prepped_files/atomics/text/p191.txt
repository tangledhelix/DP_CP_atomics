
New attention began to be focused on increasing the yield by making a
more intense beam of ions. The problem here was rather complex. Simple
reasoning indicated that one could get a more intense beam by merely enlarging
the original holes through which the beam passes. Up to a certain point
this was found useful. But beyond a certain enlargement of the openings, the
separation of the isotopes began to be reduced. The very reason for using
small openings--the need for a narrow beam to effect separation--obviously
interferes with our desire to obtain a wide, intense beam for quantity production.
Thus, a wider beam would give more material, but it would no longer be
completely separated.

Compromise was found necessary. Careful experimentation indicated the
proper enlargement of the openings that produced the beam giving the best
possible yield.

Attention was also given to increasing the yield by producing more ions
of uranium as starting material. The uranium is usually introduced into the
ionizing chamber as a gaseous compound such as uranium bromide. When
this material is bombarded with electrons we get some charged molecules
of uranium bromide as well as of uranium. Ways were found to enhance
the production of uranium ions and thus make more intense beams of
ions.

However, a new problem was introduced as the beams became more intense.
The very proximity of a large number of positively charged uranium
ions in the narrow space of a beam caused them to repel each other and thus
widen the beam. This interfered with the separation process. This effect,
known as space charge, was partially counteracted by placing other positive
charges in the vicinity to repel the uranium ions back into the beam.

Using the largest magnet . . .

By March, 1942, experimentation with these problems had increased the
yield of U235 sufficiently to make it seem feasible to try separation on a larger
scale. The largest magnet on earth was thus called upon to play its part in
the further development of the separation of U235 by magnets. Before the war,
work on a giant cyclotron had been started at Berkeley, which was intended
to be the world's largest. The magnet for this cyclotron had poles that were
more than 75 feet in diameter, with a working space of 6 feet between them.
Although the construction of this magnet had been interrupted by the war, it
was possible to complete it in a few months and use it for U235 separation.
With this magnet it was possible to operate several U235 separators within
the space between the poles at the same time.
