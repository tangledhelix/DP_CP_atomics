
Spares and the practical joker . . .

Most striking of all were the sparks that could be obtained from the friction
effect at the same time that the attractions were noticed. Familiar to us is
the practical joker who shuffles his feet across a carpet on a cold, dry day
and then touches an unsuspecting victim. Appreciable sparks can be obtained
in this manner. They are easily seen in the dark. Hair that is combed on a
dry day will crackle with tiny sparks
and become almost unmanageable
from the electrical effects.

A similar friction process in moving
air currents and clouds creates
giant sparks, which we call lightning.
The friction of a rapidly moving
car on the road may sometimes
give a considerable shock to a person
who touches the car after it has
stopped. The electricity developed
by the friction of a dirigible with the
air as it travels is sufficient to kill a
man if he should be foolish enough to touch the rope that is flung overboard
in the mooring process before it has touched the ground. Balloons and papers
can be made to "stick" to the wall if they are rubbed on clothing on a dry
winter day.

In fact, this principle of generation of electricity by friction was developed
into machines to create large sparks with which the scientists of the eighteenth
century could experiment. Various "shocking" experiments were performed
on people, and small animals and birds were electrocuted with these machines.
In one experiment a high-voltage electric charge was sent through a line of
people 900 feet long to provide a simultaneous shock to all in the line. These
electrical machines of 200 years ago were the forerunners of the modern giant
machines that are used in atom-smashing experiments. One of these will be
described later in Chapter Eight.
