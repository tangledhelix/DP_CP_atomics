
The rogues' gallery of the chemist must be enlarged . . .

As a result of hundreds of years of patient investigation of the materials
present in the earth, and after millions of individual experiments, we came
to the conclusion that there existed on this earth only 92 elements, ranging
from the lightest, hydrogen, to the heaviest, uranium. True, there were
several gaps corresponding to missing elements, which must be rare indeed
on the earth's crust. But the tabulation of all the elements is so orderly
that a chemist could predict with fair accuracy the general behavior of an
element merely from its atomic number.

Why did the listing stop with element 92? We were forced to satisfy
our curiosity with an incomplete and possibly weak argument. The naturally
radioactive elements appear for the most part at the very end of the
periodic table. Hence we concluded that elements of atomic number greater
than 92 are so highly unstable and radioactive that they must have long ago
disappeared from the face of the earth. Yet uranium, element 92, has a half-life
of about 4-1/2 billion years, certainly not a very unstable atom!

In Chapter Thirteen we discussed the exciting hunt initiated by Fermi for
elements of atomic number greater than 92. This hunt was temporarily sidetracked
with the discovery that the suspected "transuranic" atoms in uranium
bombarded with neutrons were really fission products of U235. Scientists were
extremely surprised to find that the new elements which resulted from the
splitting of the uranium atom were actually lighter elements far from uranium
in the rogues' gallery.

However, the hunt for elements beyond uranium continued. Partial success
was achieved in 1940, but it remained for members of the atomic-bomb
project not only to prove the existence of element 94 but to produce this
element in amounts corresponding to pounds, or possibly hundreds of
pounds. The story of this hunt will rank in the history of pioneering
chemical achievements with the atomic theory of Dalton and the discovery
of radium by Madame Curie.

Californians share the honors . . .

uranium with deuterons obtained from Prof. Ernest O. Lawrence's cyclotron.
