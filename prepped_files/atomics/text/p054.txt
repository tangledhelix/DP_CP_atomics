weather brought about a surprising twist that led to the discovery of radioactivity
and the beginning of the atomic age. Here is Becquerels own account
of how he came to make this epochal discovery:

"I may relate how I was led to make this observation : among the preceding
experiments some had been made ready on Wednesday the 26th and Thursday
the 27th of February and as on those days the sun only showed itself intermittently
I kept my arrangements all prepared and put back the holders in the
dark in the drawer of the case, and left in place the crusts of uranium salt.
Since the sun did not show itself again for several days I developed the photographic
plates on the first of March, expecting to find the images very feeble.
The silhouettes appeared on the contrary with great intensity."

This was indeed surprising. How was it possible for a cold material to be
giving out radiations that acted in the same way as light? Had the plate become
accidentally exposed to light by careless manipulation? Repeated trials
indicated that this was not the case. Apparently, then, the uranium compound
itself was emitting radiation that could penetrate a sheet of blacky paper without
any outside stimulus. Further, Becquerel could do nothing to affect this
peculiar emission of energy from uranium compounds. If it were a simple
chemical reaction, cooling his specimens should decrease the emission, He
found that the emission was the same at lower temperatures. Heating had no
effect. It thus became clear that this was some new property of the material
itself.

Natural radioactivity had been discovered. The hunt for the explanation
for this new puzzle then began.

The Curies become interested in Becquerel's find . . .

Becquerel's surprising observation excited the imagination of chemists and
physicists in many laboratories. Here was obviously something very fundamental.
Scientific investigators such as Rutherford and Soddy of England
began to work in this field. Soon it was found that other elements seemed
to have this same property.

Among those who became interested in this mystery were Pierre and Marie
Curie, then working in Paris. They were intrigued by the fact that the chemist
was helpless in affecting the emission of radiation from the uranium and
determined to find out more about this strange property. They proceeded to
prepare uranium compounds in the laboratory and in the process uncovered
a small, yet vitally important clue.
