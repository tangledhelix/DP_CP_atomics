The task of separating the heavy water is complicated by the extremely
small difference in boiling point. Ordinary water boils at 2120 F, while heavy
water boils at 214.6° F. Consequently, the distillation columns must be designed
with great care. Furthermore, the very small amount of heavy water in
ordinary water makes separation even more difficult.

But just as it was true that the task was inherently difficult, it was equally
true that engineering know-how concerning distillation has been carried
to a new high in this country, thanks to the efforts of our chemical engineers.
As a result, separation of heavy water by distillation was actually put into
operation early in 1944.

Heavy-water plant based on an exchange reaction . . .

Let us suppose that we shake up a bottle that contains hydrogen gas and
water. For the purpose of this example, we shall assume that the gas contains
as many molecules of deuterium (D2) as light hydrogen (H2). Further
let us assume that the water contains as many molecules of D20 as H20.
In other words, we have a system in which the proportion of heavy hydrogen
in the gas is the same as that in the liquid. Now, under ordinary conditions,
we could shake this bottle for several years without any change
occurring.

However, in the presence of a
suitable chemical agent (dubbed by
the chemist a catalyst), a surprising
process occurs. Atoms of hydrogen
from the water will exchange
places with atoms of hydrogen
from the gas. After some time, it
is observed that the heavier deuterium
atoms prefer to concentrate
in the water. The net result is that
the gaseous hydrogen now contains
more H than D and the water
contains more D than H. Practically
speaking then, we have effected
an enrichment of deuterium in the water.

Based on this principle, a plant for the production of heavy water was
constructed in British Columbia, Canada, close to a source of electric power.
