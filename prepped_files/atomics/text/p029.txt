Out of these simple electrolysis experiments came many giant chemical industries,
particularly in the field of metal production and use. Our aluminum
is made by this process of electrolysis. Copper is refined and purified by this
method. Materials such as sodium, chlorine, and lye are made in this manner.
Metal plating is also based on this simple procedure of passing an electric current
through certain solutions.

Are materials electrical in nature? . . .

And now our familiar "why" recurs. Why should the molecule of water be
split apart by the flow of electric charges? Why should metals always plate out
at the negative terminal?

The answer to these questions also provides us with the solution to the
puzzling questions we raised earlier in the chapter. Why do not all molecules
simply fly off into space as a result of their great motion? What keeps them
together in solids and liquids?

The solution began to appear toward the end of the nineteenth century.
It was found that the atoms themselves were intimately tied up with electric
charge and its effects. Atoms could become positively charged, become negatively
charged, or be neutral (with no charge at all). Positively charged atoms
remained attracted to negatively charged atoms in a stable solid structure such
as common table salt. The forces keeping atoms together in the form of a molecule,
or keeping molecules together in the form of a liquid or solid are electrical
in nature. They consist of the same
attractions and repulsions that we
noted earlier with charged objects.
Thus it was discovered that metals
always plated onto the negative
terminal because the "atom" of
metal (always found in salt solutions)
was positive, and thus was
attracted to the opposite negative
charge.
