Today, this ejection of electrons from certain metals by means of light is the
basis of a number of electronic devices such as the electric exposure meter, the
television camera, and the "electric eye."

The fact that these very same electrons could be obtained in a variety
ways and from a variety of metallic materials indicated that they existed at
all times in these materials. In other words, these electrons were closely connected
in some way with the nature of matter itself.

Was there a unit package of electric charge? . . .

By this time, it was strongly suspected that the electron represented a unit
package of electric charge. Several types of measurements had been made
which indicated that the electron had a definite amount of charge. But was this
the least electrical charge that could ever be found? In 1909, the American
physicist Millikan set about to find an answer to this question. His success was
acknowledged by the award of a Nobel prize based on this work.

The reasoning behind his clever experiments can be illustrated by this crude
analogy: Suppose thousands of tiny balls are being dragged up a hill by a
colony of ants. Unfortunately, these ants are invisible and it is therefore necessary
for you to take measurements of the speeds and to estimate the weights
of the balls. From these measurements you are able to calculate how much
lifting power is being applied. You then take a great many observations and
calculate this lifting power. To your surprise you discover that there is a certain
small unit of lifting power in some cases. In other cases there is twice
as much lifting power--three times as much--or four, five, and other exact
whole numbers. But you never find a single instance where there is 1-1/2, 2-1/4,
or any other fractional lifting power. Your conclusion is clear. The lifting
power must come in a small unit or package of one ant. You can have two
ants, three ants, or any other number of ants pulling the ball. But you cannot
have 1-1/2 or 2-1/4 ants pulling the ball up the hill.
