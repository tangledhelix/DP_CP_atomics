Let us suppose that we have a large pool of water filled to the overflow
point and our problem is to find out what happens when a barrel of water
is added at one end of the pool. If the overflow channel is located at the other
end of the pool, then of course a barrelful of water will overflow at that end.
But we have learned nothing about what really happened to the barrel of
water that we added. How far did the new water get? Where did it go?
In contrast, suppose that we had introduced some red ink. If the overflow
water is colorless, we can say that
no red ink was able to cross the
pool; it simply replaced some of
the original water of the pool.
Now suppose that the red ink appears
to concentrate near one corner
of the pool. Then we can
make some conclusions about just
how the red ink traveled. This
simple example illustrates the basis
for using radioactive atoms in
studying the chemical processes
that are constantly going on in the body. We learned about the currents in the
pool by "tagging" the water we added, by labeling it "red."

All the atoms in our body are stable--nonradioactive. If we introduce radioactive
atoms whose presence can be detected so easily by a Geiger counter, we
are tagging the atoms introduced just as clearly as the red ink was labeled in
the example above.

However, the method of using radioactive tracer atoms enables us not only
to trace the motion of atoms, but to detect minute amounts of material far
smaller than the best chemical balances can weigh. The most sensitive balances
available can measure materials weighing about 10 billionths of an
ounce. But the use of tracer techniques with radioactive atoms makes it easily
possible to detect the presence of amounts of material 100 million times smaller
than even this minute amount!
