
How would it be possible to separate this minute amount of radium fr
the barium? The solution adopted was simple in principle but very tedious
in practice. Barium chloride dissolves to a moderate extent in water, while
radium chloride is much less soluble. In practice then a hot solution of radium
and barium chlorides was allowed to cool until crystals began to form. Since
radium chloride is less soluble than barium chloride, the crystals that came
out of solution were about five times richer in radium. Thus, from a concentration
approximately equivalent to about one drop in a gallon of water,
the radium concentration was now equivalent to five drops in a gallon.

However, this technique could be repeated many times, and in each case the
product was slightly richer in radium. Finally, it was possible to prepare
compounds of radium so pure that the barium content could hardly be identified
by the spectroscope, one of the most sensitive tools of the physicist.

Radium has remarkable properties . . .

From the chemist's point of view, radium is a well-behaved element. Just
as he would have predicted from his rogues' gallery, radium is the heaviest
member of a family of elements called the alkaline earths. The lightest member
of this family is calcium, some of whose compounds are familiar to us
in the form of lime, limestone, and plaster of Paris.

On the other hand, the energy emitted spontaneously from this new "plaything"
of the physicists had remarkable properties indeed. Whereas all ordinary
substances tend to remain at the same temperature as their surroundings,
a sample of radium remains measurably warmer. In several days radium emits
more energy than can be obtained from the explosion of the same weight of
TNT. But the explosion of the TNT has used up the chemical energy latent
in this material, while the atomic energy liberated by the radium is almost
steadily maintained. In ioo years, a sample of radium will lose only 4 per cent
of its activity. Practically infinitesimal amounts of radium or other radioactive
materials dissolved in the paint of watch dials are enough to keep them glowing
in the dark for unlimited periods of time.
