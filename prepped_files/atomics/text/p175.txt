
Chapter Sixteen

Heavy Water

Flight and sabotage . . .

Two dramatic incidents high-lighted the race for the great prize of the
conquest of atomic power during the Second World War. The
Associated Press reported that in the spring of 1940 a secret scientific
mission was sent to Norway by the French government for the purpose of
bringing back a large quantity of heavy water. When the German invasion
of France took place, this extremely valuable material was carried over to
England in one of the last ships to leave Bordeaux. More precious than its
equivalent weight of gold, the 175 quarts of heavy water represented practically
the world's entire stock. Along with the heavy water came two of
France's most brilliant nuclear physicists, Halban and Kowarski. Some of
their work has already been referred to in our earlier narration of the discovery
of uranium fission.

The other incident bears directly on the Allied sabotage of German progress
in the hunt for an atomic bomb. Rjukan, Norway, is the site of a vast
hydroelectric project, which includes the largest direct-current electric plant
in the world. Enormous quantities of direct-current electric energy are required
to produce heavy water in the manner that will be described below.
