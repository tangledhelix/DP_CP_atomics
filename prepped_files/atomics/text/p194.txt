Similarly, a high-speed whirling
centrifuge containing a gaseous
compound of uranium will tend to
concentrate the heavier U238 atoms
near the outer portions of the container
and the lighter U235 at
near the center.

A small pilot plant was built
and operated successfully, but the
separation was too small to warrant
large-scale production. It was
estimated that 22,000 separate high-speed
centrifuges would be needed
to make 2 pounds of U235 in a day. Construction of large-scale plants using
this principle was therefore not undertaken.

However, there is no reason why this and other methods may not be used
some day in large-size plants. Higher speeds and more efficient centrifuges
may one day make separation practical by this method. With more time for
leisurely experimentation--now that the war is over--other methods may
come to supplant the ones found most practical during wartime. Only the
future can tell what these methods may be.

Now that we have obtained fissionable material in the form of plutonium
and U235, the question becomes one of fashioning this material into an at
bomb to play its part in winning the war. This was, after all, the only reason
for embarking on such expensive projects as the Hanford pile, the gas diffusion
plant, and the electromagnetic separation process. How was this material
utilized so as to produce the catastrophic explosions?

This brings us to the culminating achievement of the atomic-bomb project,
the actual manufacture from the fissionable material of a bomb that would
convert the potentialities of atomic energy into explosive reality.
