
The hunch turned out to be correct . . .

In order to test this idea, liquid hydrogen was allowed to evaporate until
about 4 quarts were reduced to about 20 drops of residue. Using this small
sample, the faint lines next to the main hydrogen lines in the spectrum were
found to be much more intense than in a sample of ordinary hydrogen.
Furthermore, the differences between the lines of light hydrogen and its
suspected heavy isotope checked well with the calculations they had made. As
a result of this pioneering achievement, H. C. Urey was awarded the Nobel
prize in 1934.

The discovery of heavy hydrogen led to many exciting developments.
Although merely a research tool at the time of its discovery, heavy hydrogen
was a decade later to play a dramatic role in the quest for large-scale atomic
energy. This interesting aspect of atomic adventures is described in a later
chapter. Now to return to the interest of the physicist in the deuteron.

Another high-energy projectile for the cyclotron . . .

In order to obtain high-speed deuterons for atom-smashing work, the deuterium
(heavy hydrogen) gas is introduced into the cyclotron in the same
manner as ordinary hydrogen and ionized to form deuterons. The particles
have a charge of +1 and therefore can be speeded up in the same manner
as other charged particles.

Smashing by deuteron artillery is not as efficient as neutron bombardment.
A deuteron heading toward an atomic nucleus is repelled strongly by the
positive charge on the latter in the same manner as a proton is repelled. Probably
about one deuteron in a million is effective in producing transmutations,
while most neutrons end up by taking part in some nuclear change. Nevertheless,
high-energy deuterons have become the most useful projectile in cyclotron
work. They are especially valuable because certain collisions using deuterons
will produce neutrons having energies up to about 20 M.e.v. These high
neutron energies are very difficult to attain by other means.

We may practice our atomic bookkeeping on a nuclear collision which has
found very frequent use in the cyclotron laboratory. When deuterons, speeded
up to energies of 5 M.e.v., strike a target of beryllium, neutrons are produced
that range in energy up to about 9 M.e.v.:
