
Atomic storage battery . . .

How would such "storage battery" material be made? One method would
use the fission products of U235 for this purpose. A large pile may use up
U235 at the rate of several pounds a day. In the course of the fission of these
atoms, several pounds of fission fragments are formed. Most of these fragments
are themselves radioactive and could therefore be used as atomic-storage-battery
material. Better still, radioactive material could be made from
a great many substances by placing them in the pile to be bombarded
the intense radiation and neutron beams. Or they could be run through the
pile in molten or soluble form. The latter method would make the separation
problem easy, since the liquid would simply flow out of the pile in a radioactive
state. It could then be collected in tanks and transported to distant
places.

Each solution (or perhaps pellet) would continue to liberate heat at a
rate depending only upon the half-life of the atoms. It would be possible to
select materials with appropriate half-lives for specific use. Thus, we might
use a material with a 100-day half-life for a long-term job or a shorter i-day
half-life atom for more rapid liberation of energy. The rate of heat liberation
could be easily controlled simply by adjusting the amount of radioactive
material or by diluting the solution. By using a more concentrated solution,
we could produce steam at a fairly constant rate. Still greater concentrations
would produce high-pressure steam suitable for running engines.

Complex apparatus is not required to liberate the heat. The mere presence
of disintegrating atoms in water would be sufficient. Shielding would be
required if people were expected to be near the device or if the steam were
liberated into the air. But the shielding here presents less difficulty than in
the pile where the neutrons and high-energy gammas provide the major
hazards.

The amounts of energy liberated by such radioactive samples can become
rather large. For example, consider a pound of radioactive material with a
half-life of 10 days ejecting i-M.e.v. beta particles. Over the first 10-day period,
this sample produces--continuously--an average of several hundred horsepower
in the form of heat energy!

to compete with ordinary fuels for a long time to come. But where concentrated
sources of energy are needed regardless of cost, this method may be
