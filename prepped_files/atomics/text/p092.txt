In his experiment Rutherford used alpha particles shot out from radium C.
These particles can travel about 2-3/4 inches in air before they are stopped.
This distance is called the range of the particle and is a good measure of its
original energy. When these alpha particles were allowed to bombard a
sample of hydrogen, an interesting observation was made. As a result of the
collisions, particles were shot out that had a range of 11H inches in air. What
had happened? The simplest assumption was soon found to be true. Hydrogen
atoms consist of one proton as nucleus and one external electron. The
alpha particle bullet is four times as heavy as the proton. The result of the
collision is to kick these smaller protons forward at high speeds. But this is no
transmutation of elements. A fast helium nucleus has hit a stationary hydrogen
atom. As a result, the helium nucleus is slowed down and the hydrogen
nucleus is kicked forward. As a matter of fact, simple calculations proved that
a head-on collision would be expected to give hydrogen nuclei a range of just
11H inches. The same type of calculation could be applied to a simple billiard-ball
collision. Nothing really unusual had happened.

High-speed alpha particles collide with nitrogen atoms . . .

When, however, Rutherford used nitrogen gas as the supply of target atoms,
an extremely unusual result was observed. Occasionally (recall that most artillery
practice results in many misses) particles shot out from the collision
scene with ranges of about 15.7 inches. What could these high-energy particles
be? They could not be the original alpha particles, because their range is
only 2-3/4 inches. They could not be the target nitrogen nuclei or ions, because
simple billiard-ball calculations showed that a light alpha particle could not
possibly give a heavier nitrogen atom such a great kick.
