
Checking the Einstein equation . . .

There were few rules in science so firmly entrenched as the principle of
conservation of matter. Many of the reactions performed by the chemist,
such as the burning of coal, liberated what might be considered large amounts
of energy. Despite very accurate measurements of mass before and after
reaction, no loss was ever observed by the chemist. Why was a loss in mass
never observed?

Einstein's equation provides the answer. There was a loss of mass in every
one of these reactions, but it was so infinitesimal that it could not be detected
by the most delicate chemical balances.

However, nuclear reactions liberate so much energy in many cases that
the tools of the physicist are sharp enough to detect these changes in mass.
It is a tribute to the genius of Albert Einstein that his equation was developed
from purely theoretical considerations and was presented without any experimental
proof. Decades later, it was found to apply cxacdy to the nuclear
collision processes that were beginning to interest the physicist. As a matter
of fact, it can be stated that the study of nuclear processes has been the best
experimental confirmation of the Einstein equation. Of course, the equation
was to become of practical significance years later when science found a way
to unleash large-scale amounts of atomic energy.

Let us apply the Einstein relation to one of the transmutations which we
described in an earlier section. In doing this, we must, of course, use precise
values for the atomic weights before and after because the change in mass
will be small.

Lithium bombarded with protons produces two alpha particles (helium).

Lithium + hydrogen --> 2 helium + energy
Atomic weight 7.0182 + 1.0081  = 2 X 4.0040 + energy
8.0263 = 8.0080 + energy

According to this calculation there is a loss of 0.0183 atomic units of mass.
Calculation with Einstein's equation shows that this destruction of mass
should result in the production of about 17 M.e.v. of additional energy.
Measurement of the energies of the two fast helium atoms resulting from the
collision of the proton against the lithium atom showed them to have about
8.5 M.e.v. of energy each, or a total of 17 M.e.v. together. Thus, the measurements
confirmed with great exactitude the unusual prediction of Einstein's
equation.
