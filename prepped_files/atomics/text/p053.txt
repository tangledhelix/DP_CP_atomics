
Chapter Six

Radium Introduces Atomic Energy

A most fortunate accident . . .

The history of scientific discovery is full of fortunate accidents that
lead to far-reaching revelations of the secrets of nature. Galvani notices
a frog's leg twitching, and this leads to the discovery of the electric
battery, electroplating, and the beginnings of practical uses for electricity.
Roentgen notices a peculiar glow of minerals near a covered cathode-ray tube,
and x-rays are discovered.

Radioactivity was born through a similar accidental discovery by the Frenchman
Henri Becquerel in 1896. He had become curious about the fact that certain
minerals after being exposed to sunlight were "phosphorescent" and gave
off light when they were placed in a dark room. Among these minerals were
compounds of the element uranium. Becquerel suspected that some of these
phosphorescent materials might give penetrating radiations similar to x-rays.
His usual procedure in testing this idea was to wrap some photographic film
in heavy black paper so that the film would not be "fogged" by sunlight. Then
he would place some of the mineral being tested on this covered film and expose
it to sunlight for several hours. In the case of uranium salts he observed
that the film would become fogged with some kind of light that could penetrate
the black paper. It was clear that this radiation was coming from the
uranium mineral.
