
Would a single large dose be more beneficial than small daily exposures? I
It was found that repeated small doses frequently had superior effects. This
was especially true for cancer of the throat. But pioneers in this work were
subjected to many disappointments. At first, x-ray treatment might show
beneficial results and the growth would begin to shrink. Afterward, however,
the cancerous tissue might become more resistant to this radiation.

How could the effects of x-rays be improved? The diligent studies of the
physicists had shown that x-rays were of the same character as the familiar
radiation of light but the frequency of the x-rays was thousands of times
greater. As a result, the x-rays could penetrate more deeply. If the voltage
across which the x-ray tube operates is increased, even more penetrating
radiation can be obtained. For this reason larger and more powerful machines
have been built recently for medical use. Several years ago, a 10-ton unit was
installed in the Presbyterian Hospital of New York, operating at 1 million
volts. These efforts to obtain higher voltage x-rays were amply rewarded.
Many cancers resistant to ordinary low-voltage radiation yielded to the higher I
voltage treatments. Even more powerful machines are being built. The world
anxiously awaits reports of further beneficial discoveries in this field.             !

The application of x-ray therapy has helped in several important respects.
It is sometimes preferred to radium treatment in those cases where the cancerous
tissue covers a large area having deep and indistinct boundaries. Used
in conjunction with surgery, x-rays have made possible a double-barreled
attack on the disease. Used prior to surgery, the x-rays will frequently harden
and shrink a tumor. Used after surgery, the x-rays help to kill cancerous tissue
fragments that have escaped the surgeon's knife. This approach has been
found especially effective in treating cancer of the ovary.                          j

An uncharted field for further discovery was introduced when Kerst invented
the betatron, with which extremely penetrating x-rays can be produced
from electrons ranging up to ioo M.e.v. This ingenious device was described
in an earlier chapter.

The x-ray photons kick out electrons with high velocities from the atoms
in the irradiated tissue. It is these high-speed electrons which then break
apart a large number of molecules in that region and destroy tissue. Therefore
we have reason to expect that electron-emitting radioactive samples injected
into the patient will have similar effects.

In this manner it may be possible to obtain an intense and highly localized
radiation treatment. One of our greatest hopes is that compounds will be  I
found that preferentially lodge in cancerous rather than healthy tissue. We
can then attach a radioactive atom to this compound and in this way obtain
a more effective cancer treatment.
