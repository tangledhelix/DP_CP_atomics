The advances that were made in the course of the project almost overshadow
the creation of the bomb itself. The development of chain-reacting atomic-energy
piles that can be easily controlled, the creation of sizable quantities of
the hitherto unknown element plutonium, the separation of uranium isotopes
on an undreamed-of scale and in a variety of ways, are all monumental
achievements. But the reason for this enormous effort was to prepare bombs.
In time of war, it would have been difficult to justify even these startling
advances, important to future technical development, if the military objectives
had been a failure. The problem of ensuring that all this gigantic effort at
production of fissionable plutonium and U235 would not be wasted was entrusted
to a group of scientists under the leadership of the youthful J. R. Oppenheimer.

Intensive work on the problems of the bomb itself was localized at a remote
and closely guarded site on a mesa about 30 miles from Santa Fe, N.M. The site
itself, Los Alamos, has been transformed from a desert region into one of
the best-equipped physics research laboratories in the world. It was at this
historic spot that plutonium and U235 were fashioned into bombs to usher
in the Atomic Age with dramatic suddenness.

What is an explosion? . . .

Up to the creation of atomic bombs, the world was quite familiar with a
variety of chemicals and mixtures known as high explosives. Nitroglycerin was
first prepared in 1846. In this explosive, nitrogen, carbon, hydrogen, and
oxygen atoms are linked together in an apparently stable molecule. However,
on suffering a slight shock, a small amount detonates violently, and large
enough samples cause a serious explosion. What brings about this explosion?

A combination of several factors are needed to bring about the enormous
destructive effect. When nitroglycerin or any other explosive (as well as any
other fuel) ignites, there is a rearrangement of the molecules in combination
with oxygen to produce new compounds. These new products are gaseous in
all instances of burning. Among these gases are steam (or water vapor),
carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide (and sometimes nitrogen and oxygen).
Compared to the compact liquid or solid state of the original explosive, these
gaseous products occupy far more volume than the original explosive. At the
moment of ignition in the cramped space of the shell, there is a sudden production
of these gases. At the same time there is a sudden liberation of
heat energy, which causes an enormous speeding up of the motion of the
molecules. The pressure of the enclosed gases rises suddenly to thousands of
