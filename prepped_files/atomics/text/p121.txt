radiations will lead to many fruitful discoveries. Some scientists believe that
the study of cosmic radiation may someday provide us with the mechanisms
for exploration of the nature of electrons, protons, and neutrons themselves.
Already, the study of these radiations has led to the discovery of several new
fundamental particles.

One of the intriguing features of cosmic-ray study has been the discovery
of a particle with a mass somewhere between that of the electron and the
proton. This particle, called a meson, may have a positive or a negative
charge, with a mass of about 200 times that of the electron and 710 that of
the proton. Recent studies seem to indicate that mesons may really consist
of a number of different kinds of particles that our instruments are not yet
capable of separating. The mesons have a fleeting existence of about one-millionth
of a second.

Mesons are still pretty much of a mystery. But recent experiments and theoretical
studies indicate that they may play a fundamental role in the structure
of the nucleus. Attention is therefore now focused on the problem of producing
new atom guns capable of producing particles with 300-M.e.v. energies,
sufficient to produce mesons at will from controlled atom collisions. In that
way our studies of meson properties resulting from accidental cosmic-ray collisions
can be speeded up enormously.

Further information from these studies may lead us to change our notions
about the fundamental particles that make up the nucleus and may also indicate
new and greater stores of atomic energy. Only time can tell.

Another new particle--the positron . . .

The intensive hunt for the secret of cosmic radiations brought in much
interesting data and excited much discussion. With both good fortune and
clever detective work, a new fundamental particle, the positron, was discovered
in the course of this hunt. This new particle was to play an important
part in the study of artificial radioactivity and stimulated many lines of
thought about the atomic nucleus.

as a result of countless numbers of experiments. These were the electron
and the proton. The charge on the electron was considered the fundamental
unit of negative electricity, while the proton carried an electric charge equal
in amount to that of the electron but opposite in sign. But the radical difference
between these particles was that of weight, or mass. The proton was known to
