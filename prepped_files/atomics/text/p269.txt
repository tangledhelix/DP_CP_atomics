The destructiveness of war has been greatly increased . . .

The destructiveness of war has followed, in general, the pattern of development
of man's productive ability. The use of spears in warfare accompanied
a productive system in which stone hammers and hatchets were the main
tools. Ability to kill in such a productive system was obviously limited by the
nature of hand-to-hand struggle with the crude weapons available.

However, with the development of various tools and machines, warfare
began to spread the distance between individual opponents. Skill in the use
of weapons such as the bow and arrow became more important than sheer
brute force.

The development of explosives in shells and bullets accompanied the invention
of the steam engine and other uses of chemical energy of fuels. The
destructiveness of the gun and the cannon proceeded apace with the development
of machinery for peaceful uses. The same machine that can
produce a 16-inch gun today can also produce heavy tools for making automobiles
and other peacetime products.

With the coming of the automobile, the airplane, and the radio, the arm
of warfare extended enormously across hundreds and thousands of miles--back
behind the lines into the cities and the hearts of nations. Warfare
reached out to cripple noncombatants--women and children--not simply
organized armies. Thousand-plane raids in the war recently ended leveled
whole cities. The number of men taking part in these raids was just a small
fraction of the number of soldiers and civilians killed. This organized, industrialized
type of warfare in the First World War caused approximately 30
million casualties. In the Second World War, it has been estimated that
casualties were about twice as numerous. The property damage was far
greater than in the First World War. A vast part of the continent of Europe
has been ravaged and torn.
