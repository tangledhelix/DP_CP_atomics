Now the hunt was on for these predicted waves. It did not take long to find
them. Heinrich Hertz in 1887 actually produced waves of much lower frequency
than infrared waves from a spark jumping across a gap. Moreover, he
was able to send these waves across a distance and to detect them with a receiver.
All of us now know the great importance of these radio waves in our
modern civilization.

Only a short time after the discovery of radio waves by Hertz, Roentgen
discovered x-rays, which are simply high-frequency waves beyond the ultraviolet.

The spectrum which now confronts us is far greater than our original red-to-violet
visible light. In fact, if you were to represent the frequencies by octaves on
a piano keyboard, visible light would occupy just about one octave in the middle
of the keyboard while the other frequencies would require 60 octaves, extending
on either side of the visible spectrum. New types of waves have been
added to those described above. Of even higher frequency and greater penetrating
power than the x-rays are the gamma, or radium rays, and beyond
the gamma rays are the cosmic rays, both of which we shall describe more fully
in later chapters.

Let us imagine that we have some sort of special gadget, something like
a magic radio set, which has the ability to pick up radiations and waves
ranging all the way from the lowest frequency radio waves to the very highest
frequency gamma rays. As we turn the dial the presence of the wave will be
indicated by a buzzing sound.

We start at the lowest point on the dial. There we pick up waves coming
in from ship-to-shore radio communication. A bit farther on the dial we record
various waves from broadcasting stations, each operating on its own frequency.
As we turn the dial we pass from the broadcast band into the so-called short
wave bands used by television, FM broadcasts, and amateurs. Beyond these
frequencies we catch some signals from a near-by radar station. We begin to
pick up signals from the furnace in the basement as we enter the infrared
region a bit farther on the dial. A hot flatiron in the room also causes buzzing
in our special receiver. In the daytime we also get a loud buzz from the infra-
red rays of the sun.
