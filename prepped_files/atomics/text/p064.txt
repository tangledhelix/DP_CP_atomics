To illustrate this, let us consider a sample of radium in more detail and
again look upon radium as the parent. In 1,590 years, half the original radium
has been converted to the second generation, radon gas. But as soon as some
radon is produced, it begins to break down rapidly (its half-life is only 3.82
days), and a solid element is formed. This is then a third generation member,
which is named radium A. But radium A is destroying itself at a truly
rapid pace, for its half-life is only 3 minutes. In this destruction, it shoots out
an alpha particle, giving rise to the fourth generation, called radium B. But
radium B is also breaking down, this time shooting out a high-speed electron
or beta particle! Finally, in the tenth generation, this carnage comes to a
halt. This last member of the family is called radium G but is actually lead.
For some reason still not clear, the particles making up the atoms of this last
clement have established peace among themselves and have worked out a stable
existence. Radium G is not radioactive. .

In the paragraph above, for the sake of simplicity, radium was considered to
be the parent atom. As a matter of fact, radium itself must be considered as a
sixth generation element, with uranium as the parent atom of this complicated
family. Uranium atoms have a very high survival probability. The half-life
of uranium is 4-1/2 billion years. Hence, it is not at all surprising that
Madame Curie found such minute traces of radium in uranium ore. By comparison,
radium is a "spendthrift" with a short half-life of 1,590 years, while
uranium, the parent, spends itself extremely slowly and thus outlives its offspring!

The radioactive atoms will help unravel atomic structure . . .

With just a few exceptions, all the elements in the periodic table from number
81 to 92 are known to occur in nature in the form of these unstable atoms.
Surprisingly enough, four elements considerably lighter than these have
also been found in radioactive condition in nature, but only in insignificant
quantities.

When the chemical properties of the members of this and other radioactive
families were examined, many other facts were brought to light. Among the
more interesting conclusions, the chemical identification of the members of
the family of radioactive elements and the discovery of isotopes, peculiar chemical
twins, play important roles. To understand both these effects, we turn our
attention to models of the atom.
