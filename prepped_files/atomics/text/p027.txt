the negative charge would now have an excess of negative electricity and would
thus be charged negatively. This explains the interesting observation that when
two objects are rubbed on each other one always becomes negative and the
other becomes equally positive. One has gained negative charge, the other has
lost it.

In all this early speculation there was little evidence as to the nature of these
positive and negative charges. Were they in any way connected with, or a
part of matter itself? Or were they instead some foreign material that oozed
out of all things? Not until the end of the eighteenth century were any facts
discovered that had bearing on this question. The first hint in this quest
came from an observation that at first seemed far removed from the question
of what happens when two materials are rubbed together.

A frog's leg--and electricity . . .

It was customary for doctors of the eighteenth century to prescribe such
delicacies as frogs' legs for certain ailments. Accordingly, the Italian Galvani
prepared frogs' legs for his ailing
wife. As described in one
story, his wife happened to
hold a metal scalpel near a
charged electric machine while
it was touching an exposed
nerve in the frog's leg. A spark
passed through the scalpel to
the nerve and the leg suddenly
moved.

Galvani's scientific curiosity
was aroused. He soon discovered
that he could duplicate
this effect of sudden twitching
of the dead frog's leg by touching
one end of a wire made of two different metals, such as copper and iron,
to the nerve of the leg, and the other end to a muscle. In this instance there
were prolonged convulsions of the dead leg.
