Thus light atoms have a greater effect in slowing down and stopping neutrons.

More billiard-ball effects . . .

When we describe the first atomic-energy machine, we shall see that it was
essential to find a material which could efficiently slow down fast-moving
neutrons. It is clear from the above discussion that the solution would lie
among the lighter atoms. Actually, carbon atoms, atomic weight 12, were used
for this purpose in the first machine operated in December, 1942.

The billiard-ball collision calculation for neutrons striking hydrogen atoms
shows that on the average a neutron loses 60 per cent of its energy on collision.
It is no wonder then that fast neutrons hitting the hydrogen atoms in
water or paraffin can knock out high-speed protons (the nuclei of hydrogen
atoms) from these compounds. Calculations show that a neutron is reduced to
about Yi 0,0 00 of its original energy after making only 12 collisions with
different hydrogen atoms.

Thus a great many facts led to the conclusion that the mysterious radiation
consisted of particles having a mass about equal to that of the proton but
without any electric charge. These particles are called neutrons.

As we have stated before, it appears to be more satisfactory to consider the
nucleus as made up of protons and neutrons with the number of protons equal
to the atomic number, or the charge on the nucleus. The sum of the neutrons
and protons is approximately equal to the atomic weight. For example, consider
an atom of the poisonous gas, chlorine. The atomic weight of one of the
isotopes of chlorine is 35, the atomic number is 17. The model of the nucleus
would then be 17 protons and 18 neutrons.
