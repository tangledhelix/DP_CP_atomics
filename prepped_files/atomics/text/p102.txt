Thus, the now-familiar neutron was born in the minds of investigators
long before it was actually found. But there is hardly anything more stimulating
to a curious worker than a good hunch.

Scientists sought for this as yet unproved particle in many ways. Some
sent streams of electrons through hydrogen gas, hoping that the electrons
would unite with the protons (hydrogen nuclei) to form this neutral particle.
They bombarded metallic screens with various fast particles, hoping to
detect neutral particles coming out. No result was obtained. The real trouble
was the inability of the existing measuring devices to show the presence or
the motion of such a particle. Its single most important property is its neutral
charge. This makes it impossible to deflect by electric forces. It is also
completely unaffected by magnetic fields, because it is not a moving electric
charge.

Ordinary wax provided the clue . . .

Like many other important discoveries, the neutron was discovered in a
most unexpected manner. In fact, its presence was at first misjudged to be
due to something entirely different.

In 1930, Bothe and Becker, working at Giesen, Germany, were bombarding
various elements with alpha particles from radioactive polonium and studying
the effects of these collisions. To their surprise, they observed that when
certain light elements, such as beryllium, boron, and lithium, were
barded in this manner, a high-energy radiation seemed to be produced. This
"radiation" could penetrate fairly thick sheets of metal and would pass
through an inch of lead. In fact its penetrating power appeared to be several
times greater than the most energetic gamma radiation from known radioactive
substances and it had energies as high as 14 M.e.v. It was assumed that
this "radiation" consisted of high-energy gamma rays.

In 1932, the French physicist F. Joliot and his wife Irene Curie (daughter
of Marie Curie), working in Paris, tried interposing sheets made of different
materials between the source of these radiations and their measuring instrument,
which registered a current whenever ionization was produced in their
detecting chamber. Thus their measurements indicated the ionizing effect of
the unknown radiations.
