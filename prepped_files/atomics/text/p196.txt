possible, the problem of making a bomb that would not be a "dud" was quite
a stumbling block. To get an effective explosion, it would be desirable to
have the chain reaction strongly developed in fractions of a millionth of a
second. It was dubious that this could be done. As experiments proceeded, it
became necessary to develop huge piles for the production of plutonium. Now
other major obstacles came to the fore. There was an inadequate supply of
pure uranium. Carbon in sufficient purity was not readily available in the
quantities needed. Heavy water was not obtainable in sufficient quantities for
large-scale use.

At the same time, all the huge plants needed as subsidiaries to the main
purpose of making a bomb were of new types, requiring the immediate development
of know-how with a minimum of time allotted to trying out suggested
procedures on a small scale. Everything had to go almost directly from
the planning stage to the final plant. Often one operation that hinged upon
another was undertaken even though the success of both might be in doubt.
Thus the bomb project was forced to start from scratch in practically every
important respect. And all the doubtful elements in the entire scheme had
to be pushed at breakneck speed.

All the while that this gigantic effort was being made, no one knew whether
or not it was possible even to make an atomic bomb. Many scientists, in fact,
hoped that the terrible weapon
they were seeking would turn out
to be impossible to produce or
simply would not work effectively.

But the chance that it might be
possible to make an atomic bomb
was the decisive motive force. For
it were possible and if it developed
that our enemies could
produce this bomb also, then it
might well become the deciding
factor in the war. The risk was too
great.

That left only one course open--to embark on the project and try many
alternatives with great vigor so that if there were a solution, the Allies would
have that solution first. It was under this tremendous stress that atomic energy
was lifted out of the laboratory, where it was considered a faraway possibility,
and became the most grimly realistic problem of the entire war.
