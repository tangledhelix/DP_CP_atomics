
We may summarize this theory as follows: Every element is made up of
minute particles, or building blocks, which are called atoms. All atoms of
a given element are exactly alike in all respects--in appearance and in weight.
The atom cannot be split into anything smaller. Atoms of different elements
differ in weight and properties, and this accounts for the differences in these
elements. Two or more atoms of different elements may combine to form a
group of atoms, called a molecule.

When hydrogen and oxygen burn to form water, 2 atoms of the element
hydrogen combine with 1 atom of the element oxygen to form a group of
3 atoms--a molecule of water. The water is called a compound because it is
compounded from elements. The chemist now writes the formula for water as
H2O, which simply means the grouping together into a molecule of 2 atoms
of hydrogen and 1 of oxygen. Similarly, when sodium (chemical symbol Na)
unites with chlorine (CI), 1 atom of each combines to form a molecule of
table salt (NaCl).

Explaining some of the riddles . . .

Why is there no loss or gain in weight during a chemical change? According
to Dalton's picture, chemical change simply means that the atoms have
chosen different partners. Imagine that we have a container of blue marbles
and a container" of red marbles. Weigh them. Now mix up the marbles and
weigh again. There is no change in weight, simply a new arrangement.

To use a different analogy, we might start with three boys and an equal
number of girls on a dance floor, each group separated at first. The individual
dancers correspond to the atoms. Now supply a bit of energy (in the form of
music) and three pairs of "molecules" readily form. The total weight of our
three boys and three girls has certainly not changed by virtue of the new
arrangement.

Why is the proportion of any element in a compound always the same?
Why do 23 pounds of sodium combine with 35-1/2 pounds of chlorine, always
in the proportion of 23:35-1/2? It simply depends on how much each atom
weighs and how the atoms pair up to form the molecule.

For purposes of illustration, let us take a simple molecule which consists
of an atom of element X and an atom of element Y. Let us assume that the
weight of an atom of X is 2 units, and that of Y 3 units. In a molecule of XY
the total weight is 5. In this molecule the proportion of the weights of X to Y
is 2:3. If we take 10 molecules, these will contain 10 atoms of X and 10 of Y.
The total weight of all the X atoms will be 20; of all the Y atoms, 30. The
proportion of weights of X to Y is now 20 30, which is still the same as 2 g.
