But now that we have found a way of "building up the nucleus" and have
invented instruments for finding out the weight of various atoms in a mixture,
the entire picture has become clarified. We see, also, that Prout's guess concerning
the whole number properties of atomic weights was fairly close to the
truth.

Chemical twins account for the fractions . . .

In 1932, a chemical twin or isotope of hydrogen was discovered and named
deuterium, or heavy hydrogen. The atomic number is 1 but the weight is 2,
twice that of the hydrogen atom which we have described several times in this
chapter. How would this be built up? Simply 1 proton and 1 neutron. Since
this atom occurs in ordinary hydrogen to the extent of only 1 part in 5,000,
it has practically no effect in altering the atomic weight of hydrogen as measured
by the chemist.

But now take the case of chlorine. We find, by the use of the instrument
soon to be described, that there are two important isotopes, or chemical twins,
present in a sample of chlorine gas. One has a weight of 35. The other has a
weight of 37. About three-quarters of the chlorine atoms in nature have a
weight of 35, while the remaining quarter have a weight of 37. That is why
the average atomic weight of chlorine, the weight determined by ordinary
chemical procedures, is 35.46.

We shall find that certain isotopes play a very important role in the large-scale
release of atomic energy. The chief actor here is that isotope of uranium
which has a weight of 235, commonly referred to as U235.

Sorting out isotopes in a magnetic "race track" . . .

We have seen how Thomson as far back as 1912 used electric and magnetic
forces in order to deflect positive ions and thus produce separate tracks
on a photographic film. Now suppose that two kinds of atoms of the same
element, isotopes, are present in a gas mixture. Then by Thomson's procedure
it is possible to obtain separate tracks for the ions of these chemical twins.
In this manner, they can be hunted down, identified, and studied. Instruments
