The fact that these changes in the nucleus are accompanied by extremely
high frequency photons, or gamma rays, shows that the energies involved are
far greater than for changes that take place in the outer planetary electrons.
Thus we must expect, as we probe into the structure of the nucleus, to find
energies much greater than those involved in ordinary chemical changes.
It is to the nucleus that we now turn in our quest for atomic energy. We shall
find from this point on that the center of the atom, the nucleus, will become
the focus of our attention as we unravel the threads that led to large-scale
atomic energy.

Radioactivity leads to the discovery of "chemical twins" . . .

Among the many gifts bestowed on us by the discovery of radioactivity
was a clue which helped us to understand the nature of the so-called chemical
twins, or isotopes. We have already seen that one of the pillars of Dalton's
atomic theory was that all the atoms of an element must have exactly the same
weight. Thousands of chemists attempted to check this assertion during the
nineteenth century. Oxygen atoms were arbitrarily assigned a weight of 16.00
and the weights of all other atoms were expressed on this basis. It was soon
established that carbon, regardless of the compound in which it was found
or the region of the world from which it was obtained, had a definite atomic
weight close to 12.0. Atomic weights were obtained for dozens of other elements.
At the end of the nineteenth century, it appeared that Dalton's assertion
was absolutely valid.

This very pleasant state of affairs was rudely jarred in the early years of
the twentieth century by the discovery of the radioactive families of elements.
One such family, starting with uranium as the parent and radium G as the
final generation, was described in the last chapter. The chemical properties
of this final element, radium G, were identical in every respect with those of
ordinary lead. Tests with the spectroscope revealed that radium G gave lines
in the spectrum practically identical with those from ordinary lead. Hence it
was certain that radium G and lead were one and the same element.

A real surprise was in store for the curious investigators who measured
the atomic weight of the lead that was formed in this manner from uranium.
