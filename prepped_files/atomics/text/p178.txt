
Heavy water is an excellent moderator . . .

As we pointed out in Chapter Fourteen, in our discussion of chain-reacting
piles using ordinary uranium, the lighter the atom the more suitable it is for
slowing down neutrons. We also noted that ordinary hydrogen is unsuitable
because of its tendency to act as a neutron robber. Deuterium, next in weight
to the lightest hydrogen atom, does not have this same ability to absorb neutrons
and therefore becomes the best material known for slowing down
neutrons. Since oxygen also has little tendency to rob neutrons, the compound
heavy water (D2O) becomes ideal moderator material. There is only one
drawback, but it is a most serious one. Production facilities for this material
arc very limited, and its expense make it rather prohibitive for use at this
time.

Nevertheless, as soon as it was realized that heavy water might be suitable
as a moderator for a chain-reacting pile, this laboratory curiosity became
inexorably drawn into the atomic-bomb race. According to Smyth:
"The British, particularly J. Chadwick, were convinced that a U235 chain
reaction could be achieved. They
knew that several kilograms of
heavy water a day were being produced
in Norway and that Germany
had ordered considerable quantities
of paraffin to be made using heavy
hydrogen; it was difficult to imagine
a use for these materials other than
in work on the uranium problem.
They feared that if the Germans got
atomic bombs before the Allies did,
the war might be over in a few
weekj!' (Emphasis ours.)
As a result, it became necessary to work along another of the "impossible"
lines of attack on this problem--the production of larger quantities of heavy
water. It was no longer a question of preparing a few drops of this liquid
for use as a tracer in chemical reactions and as an aid to biology and medicine.
Heavy water had assumed a grimmer role.

Research at Columbia on possible methods of large-scale concentration of
heavy water was begun early in 1941 under the direction of H. C. Urey.
Studies were also carried out in the Frick Chemical Laboratory of Princeton
University. Two major methods were used for the concentration of deuterium.
