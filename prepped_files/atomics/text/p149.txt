
Chapter Fourteen

First Atomic-energy Machine

From the time of the demonstration of the theoretical possibility of
an atomic-energy machine in 1940 to the time of the dropping of
an atomic bomb on Hiroshima in 1945, an intense black-out curtain
kept the question of atomic energy in utmost darkness. From this thickly
veiled secrecy, the atomic bomb burst forth as the most dramatic and
terrible proof that this theoretical possibility could be made into actuality.
What had happened in these five short wartime years to bring the subject
of atomic energy from the desks and laboratories of the physicists to
the table of the general staff of the Army? How did these scientists master
the numerous baffling practical problems that must have arisen? How did
they manage to do the job in so short a time?

The official report of the atomic-bomb project, written by Henry De
Wolf Smyth of Princeton, details the trials and successes of the project.
The report describes how the 2-billion-dollar gamble finally ended in complete
and overwhelming success. It tells the dramatic story of the hunt that
continued under the black-out curtain for solution of problems which were
already clear to scientists in 1940.
