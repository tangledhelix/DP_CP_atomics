SPEED-UP TRACK

The diagram shows a speed-up track for a positively charged projectile
trying to get at the nuclear target, represented at the bottom of the track. The
speed-up track in this case might represent the accelerating tube of a Van de
Graaff generator or the high speed imparted to a particle by a cyclotron. At the
bottom of the tube--or speed-up track--the particle has acquired a considerable
amount of energy with which to climb "over the nuclear wall." Suppose we
start with the projectile at A. It has an initial energy of i M.e.v. As it begins
to climb the wall leading to the target, it experiences strong repulsive forces.
As a result it is stopped completely as soon as it has climbed to its original
height. In other words, there is little likelihood that this particle will penetrate
to the target by climbing over the hill. The same would be true for a 2-M.e.v.
proj ectile. It will climb up to point J? and then start to slide back.

If the particle has an energy of 3 M.e.v., however, it can go over the top and
penetrate into the nucleus. A different nucleus has now been produced and
changes may be expected. The new nucleus may be radioactive.

If the projectile has an energy of 10 M.e.v. instead of the required 3 M.e.v.,
then it will enter the nucleus carrying with it an additional 7 M.e.v. of energy.
