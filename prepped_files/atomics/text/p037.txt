that there was a fundamental unit of positive electricity equal to the unit of
negative electricity. The evidence for this became very convincing since the,
charges carried by other ions were either equal to this fundamental unit or
exactly twice as great, three times as great, etc.

In addition Thomson found that in a great many cases the weight of the
ions in the positive rays was equal to the weight of the atoms making up
the gas. In these cases it seemed very clear that the ions in the positive rays
were simply atoms that had lost one, two, or several electrons. But these lost
electrons must have contributed very little to the weight of the atom. Thomson
noticed that the hydrogen ion having a charge of +1 weighed about 1,850
times as much as did the electron.

Thomson proposes a model for the atom . . .

Here were a great number of clues. How could they be tied together?
Where did the positive and negative particles come from? At this point,
Thomson boldly presented a model for the atom. Since his time, the atomic
picture has been brought into much sharper focus, yet his contribution was
a brilliant pioneering effort. And as is the case with many startling developments,
his model was exceedingly simple.

According to this model, the hydrogen atom consisted of a single positive
charge and a single negative charge of equal amount. Since the positive charge
seemed to carry with it all the weight of the hydrogen atom in the positive-ray
experiments, he assumed that the main body of the hydrogen atom is a
positive particle, which he called a proton. Since he could never find any positive
particle in all his experiments that was lighter than this positive ion of
hydrogen, he assumed it to be a basic particle of matter, along with the electron.
Further he assumed that in order to make the hydrogen atom neutral
in charge as it is in ordinary hydrogen, it is necessary to add to it a negative
charge exactly equal in amount.

At this point the electron fits into the picture beautifully. If the electron has
a negative charge exactly equal to the positive charge, then the addition of one
electron to the proton would make one neutral atom of ordinary hydrogen.
The extremely light weight of the electron compared with the weight of
the proton (1/1,850 as much) accounts for the fact that no appreciable difference
in the weight of a neutral hydrogen atom was found when it became
a single positive ion. Thomson declared that the positive hydrogen ion was
produced by the tearing away of the electron from the hydrogen atom. In all
cases the electron must be pulled away by some force, because the attraction of
the positive proton and the negative electron keeps them strongly bound together.
