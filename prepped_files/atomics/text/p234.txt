It will take a lot of digging and prospecting to find that apparently limitless
supply of uranium 235 that will last us for billions of years. But prospecting
has hardly begun. Up to now uranium has had extremely limited use for
nonessential purposes. It was therefore mined only in convenient locations.
Now there is an enormous incentive to find it. It is quite probable that within
a few years the sources of supply will be enormously expanded and our known
uranium resources extended to meet a great many needs.

Thus, we need not worry about finding sufficient uranium on earth. Our
main problem is the removal of the threat of wars so that we may rapidly reap
the bounty of peaceful atomic-energy use.

Controlled atomic power--how soon?

Many of the facts needed to attempt to answer this question have already
been given. Another factor is the intensity with which the government will
subsidize and stimulate the work. Government help will probably be needed
at the start because the first power plants will have all the "growing pains"
associated with any entirely new project and will be very expensive. A power
pile is under construction at Oak Ridge, Tenn. Under this great impetus, it
appears that the first practical power plants run by atomic energy will be
large plants located in regions reasonably far away from homes and factories.
Companies long connected with the power industry have begun to take an
active part in the operation of atomic-energy piles.

Estimates have been made of the costs of power production by atomic-
energy piles at the present moment. One such estimate by the engineers of
the Monsanto Chemical Co. gives the construction cost for a 75,000-kilowatt
atomic-energy plant as 25 million dollars, in comparison with a 10-million-dollar
cost for an equivalent power plant operated by steam produced
coal. However, the operating costs for the coal-burning plant would be greater
because of the constant consumption of large quantities of fuel, while the
atomic-energy plant contains the uranium fuel within itself for a long period
of time. As a result the cost of production of electric power from the atomic-energy
plant is estimated to be 0.8 cent per kilowatt-hour, as compared with
0.65 cent for the coal-burning plant. This is just the cost of production of the
electric power and does not include the cost of transmitting it to the user.
