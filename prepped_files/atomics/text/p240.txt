Radioactive materials in industry . . .

Still another use for such atomic storage batteries would be as ionizing
agents for various industrial purposes. For example, one of the sources
fire hazards in industry is the production of sparks by static electricity generated
by moving belts and other machine parts. This may cause the ignition
of dust or vapors and thus start serious fires. A piece of radioactive material
placed near the moving part would produce a great many alpha, beta, and
gamma rays which would ionize the near-by air by ripping off electrons fr
molecules. These charged molecules would then be attracted to oppositely
charged machine parts near by and so would neutralize the charge. The passage
of sparks would thus be reduced considerably and the danger of fire
from this source eliminated.

A similar application might be found in some industrial processes where
"sticking" of material because of electric charges generated by friction might
be a problem. Fibers and papers which are in motion and rubbing against
other surfaces tend to become charged, so that they are then attracted to oppositely
charged surfaces or repelled by like charges. The resulting tendency
to fouling of the motion of the fibers or papers may seriously interfere with
the industrial process. The presence of a radioactive material would tend to
eliminate this defect by ionizing the air and neutralizing the charges produced.
This might become an important use in printing, where static electricity
causes such a problem.

Another important use for the atomic storage battery might be for the
production of "cold light." Luminous clock dials represent such a use of radioactive
materials. Small amounts of radioactive materials have long been placed
in paints, together with certain activating chemicals, to produce luminosity.
This is different from the "phosphorescent" paints, which glow only after
being exposed to light and which gradually decrease in light-producing power
until the next exposure. The luminous watch dial glows all the time, whether
exposed to light or not. It receives its energy for light production from the
radiations given off by the radioactive atoms. It seems reasonable to expect
that the production of large quantities of cheap radioactive materials may
make it feasible to produce greater luminosities at less cost than is possible
now. Thus, a permanently glowing sign that operates without electric current
becomes a possibility. Who knows but that some such scheme for lighting
homes and factories may one day come to replace our present methods of securing
illumination?
