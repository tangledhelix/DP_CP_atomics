
Chapter Thirteen

Splitting Uranium Atoms

By 1934 we knew many things about the nucleus of the atom and about
atomic projectiles. Several fundamental building blocks of the atom
were already familiar tools--electrons, protons, neutrons, and positrons.
Natural radioactivity was well understood and parent-daughter relationships
among atoms had been worked out. The laboratory worker could
produce high-speed particles equivalent to many million electron volts by
means of various atom guns. Using these atom guns and their high-speed
projectiles, as well as the bullets from naturally radioactive materials, it was
possible to chip off very small portions of atomic targets. In this way man was
able to change one element into another for the first time.

Alchemic marvels surpassing even the imagination of the early workers
were everyday laboratory occurrences. Using the same tools, the pioneer work
of the Joliots in France proved that it was possible to make radioactive elements
in the laboratory. But it was clear to all who followed these experiments
that only small-scale atomic energy was involved. In general, much more
energy had to be used up in the laboratory than was obtained as a result
of the nuclear reactions. An "energy accountant" would conclude that the
physicists were definitely in the red on this score. The number of high-energy
particles produced as a result of atom chipping was still quite insignificant.

The situation of the scientists has been described by Einstein as "like a
blindfolded man shooting birds in a country where there are not many birds."
