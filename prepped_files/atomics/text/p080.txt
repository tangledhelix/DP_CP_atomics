amount of energy possessed by the particle.

In the case of an electron, an energy of i electron volt ( 1 e.v.) corresponds to
a speed of about 360 miles per second. But since the electron volt is an energy
unit--just as is a calorie or a kilowatt-hour--it can be used to express the
energy of any charged particle, any uncharged particle, and even photon
bullets of radiant energy such as x-ray photons. Here are a few signposts
that are worth recalling whenever energy is expressed in these units.

When an atom of carbon "burns" in air to give a molecule of carbon dioxide,
the heat energy liberated for each atom of carbon burned is about 4 electron
volts. We give this example to emphasize the fact that the energies liberated
during ordinary chemical reactions are only a few electron volts. The energy
necessary to rip apart a molecule into two fragments (to ionize a molecule)
may be in the neighborhood of 30 e.v. Now compare these small values with
the energies in processes involving the nucleus of the atom. These energies are
so great that we have found it more convenient to use the unit million electron
volts, abbreviated M.e.v. The splitting of the uranium atom, from which we
obtain large-scale atomic energy, involves the liberation of 200 million electron
volts (200 M.e.v.) per atom.

These figures help us to understand why we must magnify our concepts
by a factor of millions when we shift our gaze from the energies involved in
ordinary chemical reactions to those concerned with the nucleus of an atom.
That is why we shall be using M.c.v. much more frequently than e.v. In order
to penetrate into this nucleus, we shall want to impart to our charged projectiles
high speeds--possessing energies of the order of millions of electron
volts. A proton approaching an atomic nucleus with an energy of 1 M.e.v.
will more effectively penetrate the "repulsive region" close to the nucleus than
one with an energy of only 100 e.v.
