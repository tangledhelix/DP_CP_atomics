A mysterious beam of particles . . .

But really exciting effects were observed by the Englishman Crookes a
1878. He continued to pump out the air from the tube until practically all the
glow coming from the inside of the tube had disappeared. However, evidence
that something interesting was going on came from the glass of the tube,
which now began to glow. Although he tried various schemes to find out what
was going on inside the dark tube, he met with little success. Soon others
began to experiment with this "Crookes tube." Many lines of attack were
pursued. It now began to appear that a stream of particles left the negative
terminal of the "empty tube" and traveled toward the positive terminal. What
led to this conclusion? What kind of particles were they?

A light paddle wheel was placed inside the tube. It began to spin around in
such a direction that particles coming from the negative terminal must have
been striking it. If zinc sulfide powder,
known for its ability to fluoresce,
was placed inside the tube,
then the path of the beam streaking
down the tube became illuminated.
Now if oppositely charged plates
were placed above and below the
beam, the straight beam became
curved. In every case the beam
curved toward the positive plate and away from the negative plate. Thus it
would seem that the beam consisted of negative particles. A magnet brought
near the beam also caused it to swerve, again in a manner which indicated
that the beam was composed of negative particles. The name assigned to a
negative terminal is the cathode.
Therefore, these mysterious rays
were called cathode rays and the
empty tube called a cathode-ray tube.

All the available evidence indicated
that the cathode rays consisted
of negatively charged particles. Since

up the negative terminal of the empty tube, these particles were probably part
