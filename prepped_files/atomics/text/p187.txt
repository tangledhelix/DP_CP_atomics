was to contain 99 per cent U235--or 990,000 U235's in 1 million molecules--the
gas would have to be allowed to diffuse through a large number of successive
barriers. Calculation revealed that the staggering number of 4,000 barriers
might be required. This figure is astonishing even to many of our most experienced
engineers. The final plant constructed to do this job was carrying
on the largest continuous chemicophysical process in the world.

Specifications for the barrier material were hard to meet . . .

Consider the fantastic requirements that had to be fulfilled by a suitable
barrier material through which the gases were to diffuse. First, in order to
permit large volumes of gas to go through, the diffusers had to be very porous.
Thus, there had to be billions of holes in the barrier material. But large holes
might allow the gas molecules to sweep through in a mass, in the same manner
that paste can be squirted from a tube. This would result in a very poor isotope
separation. The porous material used in the barrier should therefore have
very few holes that are larger than one-millionth of an inch. Furthermore, these
holes must not become plugged up as a result of chemical attack by the gas.
In addition, the barrier has to be strong enough to withstand a pumping pres-
sure of 15 pounds per square inch, great enough to crush a strong gallon can.
But finally, putting a cap on all these difficult requirements, the porous material
had to be manufactured in large quantities corresponding to many acres, with
uniform quality.
