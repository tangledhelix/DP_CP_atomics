fluid seemed to come out of one material when rubbed with another. The
scientists of this period therefore concluded that this electric fluid was present
at the start in the object that was rubbed. To describe the fact that the rubbed
material was in a different condition, the expression electric charge was applied.
A rod of glass rubbed on cloth and showing these electrical effects was
then said to be charged.

One of the most unusual properties of this electric charge was a repelling
force that was observed under certain circumstances. When two different
materials were charged by rubbing and brought near each other, they might
attract in some cases and push away, or repel, in other cases. Whether they
attracted or repelled seemed to depend solely upon the materials used. But
a repulsion was always observed when two pieces of the same material, charged
in exactly the same way, were brought near each other. Thus if a piece of
hard rubber or bakelite is rubbed on fur and suspended by a string so as to be
free to rotate, another bakelite rod rubbed on fur brought near it will cause
a distinct repulsion.

How explain these unusual facts? What happens when materials are rubbed
on each other to cause these attractions and repulsions and the sparks? As
the various detectives brought in their clues, others tried to piece them together
to form plausible theories to explain these facts.

Ben Franklin helps out . . .

The dual nature of electric charge--sometimes resulting in attraction and
sometimes in repulsion--caused early experimenters to ascribe two different
conditions to electrical effects. To some it seemed as if there were two different
kinds of electricity. According to this view attractions resulted when
two objects brought near each other had different charges, and repulsions when
they possessed like charges. Normal, neutral materials were viewed as having
equal amounts of these different electric fluids.

Ben Franklin, on the other hand, visualized one kind of electric fluid with
a certain "normal share" existing in all materials in the neutral state. It is to
him that we owe the terms positive (+) and negative (--). An excess of this
electric fluid was a positive state, and a loss of electric fluid gave rise to a negative
state.

are two opposite kinds of electricity, one positive and the other negative. An
equal amount of each exists normally in all materials in the neutral state. Any
