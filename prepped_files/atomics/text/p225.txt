
Harnessing the atomic power of chain-reacting piles . . .

The uranium-carbon piles built during the war contained graphite and
ordinary uranium in which the content of U235 was Ko of 1 per cent. Chain-reacting
piles can be built for large-scale liberation of atomic energy using
uranium or plutonium. Element number 90, thorium, is also of great interest
to us in connection with chain-reacting piles. Thorium in a pile will act like
uranium 238 in that it will tend to capture neutrons and convert itself into a
new atom. In this case the thorium 232 becomes uranium 233. And this U233
has the same property of splitting and liberating neutrons when bombarded
with neutrons. Therefore, U233 may become bomb material or enrichment
material. Since thorium is more plentiful than uranium, it seems likely that
future piles may operate with slugs of uranium and thorium rather than
with uranium alone.

But as far as we know, uranium is the only natural material with which
we can start a pile. The plutonium, or the U233 produced from the pile, are
secondary products that do not occur in nature. Consequently uranium remains
the key to all practical development of atomic energy as far as we can
see at the present moment.

During the operation of a pile, large amounts of energy are liberated in
the form of heat. In 1944, the uranium-carbon pile at Clinton, Tenn., was
furnishing heat at the power rate of 1,800 kilowatts. This heat was carried
away by an air stream flowing past the heated uranium slugs. The uranium-carbon
piles at Hanford, Wash., were designed to operate at a considerably
higher power rate in order to create sufficient plutonium for the production of
atomic bombs. Piles producing about 2 pounds of plutonium per day might
require the removal of heat energy at the rate of anywhere from 500,000 to
1,500,000 kilowatts. In the case of the Hanford piles, the fast-flowing waters
of the Columbia River were used to carry away the heat as a waste product.
Efforts were not directed toward harnessing this heat energy, the primary
jective of the wartime piles being the creation of plutonium. Pausing to
design power-harnessing equipment would have meant serious delay in the
production of plutonium.

How would the energy of a pile be used? . . .

radiation. The use of a pile for heating homes and factories would be the
