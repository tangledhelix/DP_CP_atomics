Watching the boring of a cannon . . .

An important advance in our understanding of heat, and ultimately of th
atomic theory, was made by Benjamin Thompson (Count Rumford). While
supervising the boring of a cannon at Munich in 1798, he was impressed by
the tremendous amount of heat developed by the friction of the boring instrument.
He arranged to have the cannon bored while it was submerged in
cold water. The cannon and boring instrument became hot, and then the
water boiled. "It would be difficult to describe the surprise and astonishment
expressed in the countenances of the by-standcrs on seeing so large a quantity
of cold water heated and actually made to boil without any fire."

This was quite surprising, particularly in view of the theory of heat that
was popular then. Heat was viewed as a material substance, an invisible,
weightless fluid known as caloric which was supposed to flow out of hot
objects and into near-by cold objects. But how explain the apparently inexhaustible
supply of this caloric arising from cold water and cold metals?
Rumford provided a partial answer when he surmised that the motion of the
boring instrument produced some kind of motion inside the metal of the
cannon which manifested itself as heat. Thus, according to Rumford, heat
was not a material substance but a motion of some kind.

What is it that has the motion? Viewing a hot liquid, one can see circulating
motion caused by the heat. But solids showed no such motion, even when
viewed under the most powerful microscopes.

Is heat a motion of molecules? . . .

Sir Humphry Davy carried out more experiments on the production of heat
by friction and in 1799 made the shrewd guess that "heat . . . may be defined
as a peculiar motion, probably a vibration, of the corpuscles of bodies." But
Davy could shed no light as to just what these corpuscles were.
