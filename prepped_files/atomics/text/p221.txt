We imitate the sun, but in reverse . . .

Thus the great "dead storage" reservoir of material which we can burn
to obtain useful heat energy consists of our coal and oil deposits, obtained
indirectly from the sun's energy. Trees and plants and the water from rain
constitute our current active storehouse. This immediate supply will last just
as long as we permit our forests and vegetation to grow and maintain our
reservoirs and dams. Short of unexpected changes in the sun's radiation or
sudden variations in the earth's geography and climate, we can expect many
billions of years of supply of energy from this current source.

But our dead storage supply, valuable mainly as concentrated energy for use
in homes, factories, transportation, and power plants, will not last indefinitely.
Only a few centuries of oil supply and several thousand years of coal supply
remain in the earth's surface. From a long-range point of view, new energy
resources are essential.

Atomic energy enters the picture at this point. We can go directly to the
same storehouse from which the sun itself gets its supply. However, there is
an important difference in the way in which this energy is tapped. The sun
is able to combine light hydrogen atoms to form helium atoms. In this process
the 1 per cent loss of mass is converted into energy. This is a fusing of atoms,
not a splitting.

On the other hand, mankind is now working at the splitting of heavy elements
such as uranium into lighter elements and converting some of the mass
lost in the process into energy. We are thus operating at the other end of the
rogues' gallery of the elements. The net result of both processes is to convert
the light and heavy atoms found in nature into the more stable atoms of intermediate
weight. From a long-range point of view there is enough uranium
in the earth's crust to permit us to work this splitting process for many centuries.
