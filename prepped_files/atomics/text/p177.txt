
Concentrating heavy water by means of electric energy . . .

It would appear to be a very difficult task indeed to separate this minute
amount of deuterium from ordinary water. Isotopes resemble each other so
closely that separation is always a tedious task. But in 1932 and 1933 the surprising
fact came to light that electricity could be harnessed to accomplish
this task. Water is, of course, a very stable chemical substance. It can be
heated to 1,000° F without suffering any appreciable decomposition to form its
constituent elements, hydrogen and oxygen. However, when wires are connected
to the terminals of a storage battery or other source of direct current
and inserted into a dilute solution of a salt (or an acidic or caustic material),
water is readily decomposed into hydrogen and oxygen gas in the process
known as electrolysis, as described in Chapter Three. A large variety of commercial
electrolysis cells for the production of hydrogen and oxygen from
water have been used in the chemical industry for many years. It has been
the customary practice to use water solutions of caustic soda (lye) in these
cells.

In 1932, Urey and Washburn made the discovery that samples of water
obtained from the solutions used in these cells were richer in deuterium than
samples of ordinary water. The lighter hydrogen molecules are liberated more
rapidly at the negative terminal than the heavier deuterium molecules. The
net result is that deuterium tends to remain behind and concentrate in the cell
solution. Gilbert N. Lewis of the University of California, together with other
chemists, showed in 1933 that the continued electrolysis of water would leave
a residual liquid that is practically pure heavy water (D2O).

Soon many laboratories in this country and elsewhere began to prepare small
quantities of heavy water, ranging from a few drops to a fraction of a quart.
The process is tedious, inasmuch as the electrolysis of 1,000 quarts of water
is necessary to produce just a few spoonfuls of almost pure heavy water. To
accomplish this electrolysis requires electric energy equivalent to operating
a 120-watt bulb for about 400 years. At 3 cents a kilowatt-hour, this amount
of electric energy would cost about $13,000, so you can easily sec how fast the
cost runs up.

For this reason, the economical manufacture of heavy water by electrolysis
could be carried out only in regions of very cheap and plentiful electric
power. The hydroelectric stations in Norway were found to be ideally suited
for this purpose. By 1940 several hundred quarts of heavy water had been
made, principally in these Norwegian plants. The total supply in the United
States at that time was only a few quarts.
