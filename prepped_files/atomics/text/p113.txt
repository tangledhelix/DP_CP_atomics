
Chapter Eleven

Atoms and the Universe

Offhand, one could not imagine two fields of scientific exploration
more unrelated than astronomy and the atom. One deals with the
infinite, the other with the infinitesimal. There would seem, at
first glance, to be no connection between the two. Yet pioneering discoveries
made by astronomers have helped to lift atomic physics to its present high
state of development.

Suppose that an immense fog persisted at all times in the atmosphere above
us, which effectively blocked out our view of the planets and stars. It is highly
probable that, as a result of our inability to see the heavens, our struggle for
scientific knowledge would be retarded by many centuries. It was in an attempt
to explain the motions of the planets that Newton developed the basic laws
of motion. Today every engineer uses these laws in building our modern
automobiles, airplanes, bridges, and skyscrapers. These very same laws are
used in the atomic billiards game with which we pry open the secrets of the
atom. They were used by Millikan in measuring the charge on the electron, by
Einstein in developing the equivalence of mass and energy, by Rutherford in
probing the structure of the atom.

Moreover, it was from the sun that we had early inklings of the existence
of atomic energy. It was on the sun that helium was first discovered with
the spectroscope.
