Radioactive iodine and the thyroid gland . . .

An important use of radioactive isotopes has recendy come to light with
the disclosure of successful experimentation in the treatment of thyroid conditions
with radioactive iodine 131, having a half-life of about 8 days, which
is present in the pile as one of the fission products of uranium.

The thyroid, one of those small but highly important organs controlling
vital body functions, consists of a pair of glands lying near the windpipe in
the neck. The functioning of this gland involves the production and use of
an active compound containing iodine, called thyroxin. A deficient functioning
of this gland is called hypothyroidism and frequently results in great loss
of mental and physical vigor, loss of hair, and thickening of the skin. On the
other hand, excessive activity of the thyroid gland results in the condition
known as hyperthyroidism and frequently leads to a nervous and easily
excitable state of mind, increased rate of over-all body function, and accelerated
heart activity.

Drs. Hamilton and Soley, working at the University of California, were
among the pioneers to use radioactive iodine as a tracer in studying these
diseases of the thyroid. Patients were fed about one one-hundred-millionth of
an ounce of radioactive iodine in the form of sodium iodide. Since the thyroid
gland is so close to the skin and the radiations are quite penetrating, it is only
necessary to place a Geiger counter over the skin of the patient's neck to measure
the intake of radioactive iodine by this gland. Hypothyroid cases could
be diagnosed by noting that very little iodine was taken up and used by the
gland. Persons with overactive thyroids showed a characteristic rapid iodine
intake followed by an equally rapid discharge. Occasionally this novel method
will indicate errors in diagnosis from ordinary clinical tests.

Other exciting results can be obtained by making sections of the thyroid
gland "take their own photograph." Before surgery is performed, the patient
receives a dose of radioactive iodine. Thin slices are now cut from this thyroid
tissue and held in contact with a photographic film. The radioactive iodine
in the tissue exposes the film as if it were a source of light and as a result the
exact location of the iodine in the tissue can be clearly identified.
