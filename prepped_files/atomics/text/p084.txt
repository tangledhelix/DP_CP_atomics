The key to the successful operation of the cyclotron is that the larger semicircle
is covered in the same time because the speed is correspondingly greater.
As soon as the proton returns to the center line again, the voltage is switched
once more. Terminal A now becomes negative and attracts the proton back
across the space between the boxes, giving it another increase in speed, now
corresponding to an energy of 30,000 electron volts. The magnetic field then
swerves the proton in a larger circle (because of its increased speed) and brings
it back to the center line, where the switching of the charge on the two halves
of the box again pulls the proton across the space and still further increases
its speed (now corresponding to 40,000 electron volts). The proton thus
travels in a gradually increasing spiral, faster and faster with each return to
the center line, until it is traveling at speeds up to thousands of miles per
second. These high-speed particles are then permitted to strike the target
material placed near the outside of the cylindrical box and smash into the
atoms of the target. The proton plows through the outer electron rings of the
atom and, despite the repulsion of the like positive charge of the nucleus, can
occasionally force its way into the nucleus.

Building larger merry-go-rounds . . .

It is obvious that the arrangement described must be designed with great
precision if it is to work. The particles are traveling with speeds up to thousands
of miles per second near the rim of the split box. The length of the
path is only a few feet, and the time for the particle to come around and back
to the center line is therefore measured in millionths of a second. The alternating
charges on the plates must then be changed millions of times per
second! At the same time the magnetic force must be powerful enough to
swerve the particle around in a short circle despite its tremendous speed. It is
a tribute to the precision of modern science that all these factors could be
combined in proper amount to obtain the desired result.

The cyclotron used in California in the early forties weighed about 200 tons
and could produce protons having an energy of 8 M.e.v. and high-energy alpha
particles approaching 40 M.e.v. It was for his achievement in originating and
developing the cyclotron that Lawrence was awarded the Nobel prize in 1940.
Prior to the Second World War, about 35 of these cyclotrons had been constructed
by the major scientific centers in many nations.
