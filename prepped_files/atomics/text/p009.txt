Aristotle explained these and other changes in materials by setting forth an
important theory of elements. There were, he said, four basic material elements--earth,
air, fire, and water. To these he added a spiritual element which,
if properly used, could change one substance into any other. All materials, he
claimed, were made of these four elements in different proportions. Thus, a
human being contained all four elements. He breathed air. He drank water.
He ate food. His body was warm.

This explanation is unsatisfactory to us today, but it contained an important
germ of truth that is essential in our understanding of materials and the
changes that take place in them. The idea that all substances are made of
simpler basic materials, or elements, is an important link in the chain of
our knowledge. Aristotle was wrong in his selection of earth, air, fire, and
water as basic elements, but he was right in postulating that elements existed.

Given impetus by Aristotle's elements, the alchemists of the Middle Ages
bent their efforts toward the quest for the imaginary "philosopher's stone"
that could magically turn baser metals into gold. For about 1,000 years these
alchemists labored at purifying metals; they mixed together powders and
potions of all kinds and colors in order to transmute common materials into
gold. The gold makers practiced their fraudulent rites at the courts of kings,
who readily succumbed to the lure for treasure. Their attempts to make gold
failed miserably, but their labors were not at all in vain. From countless numbers
of experiments were gathered many facts about the behavior of materials
when mixed together. It is for this reason that the alchemists are occasionally
referred to as the grandfathers of present-day chemistry. Working individually
and guarding their secrets jealously, they nevertheless stored up a body of
knowledge about chemical changes. But during this interval and up to
the eighteenth century little insight was gained into the nature of matter itself.

"A jew architects and many workers" . . .

It has been aptly said that the cathedrals of science were "built by the efforts
of a few architects and of many workers." We can here merely scan the work
of some of these architects which led to the edifice of atomic theory designed
by John Dalton early in the nineteenth century.

The Irishman Robert Boyle, working in the middle of the seventeenth century,
was a careful experimenter who chided those who reasoned in terms of
dogma and mysticism. Following the path laid by Democritus, he believed
that matter was composed of tiny particles, or atoms. Quite in line with our
present view, Boyle applied the term element only to a simple substance that
could not be broken down in the laboratory into even simpler substances.
