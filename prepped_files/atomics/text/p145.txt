During 1939, the scientists of all nations were still free to pursue the answers
to these questions raised by uranium fission and to publish their results immediately.
Great spurts were made as a result of this world-wide cooperative
effort. But almost simultaneously with the beginning of the Second World
War in September, 1939, came the depressing realization that developments
in this field might lead to superweapons of decisive character. As a result, there
prevailed over the investigation of uranium fission a blackout that was to be
partially lifted only when the first atomic bomb fell on the Japanese city of
Hiroshima on August 5,1945.

However, during the interval between the announcement of the discovery
of fission and the imposition of rigid secrecy, partial answers to many
of the questions that arose had been widely publicized.

Foremost among these questions was the possibility that other elements besides
uranium might be split in the same manner. To answer this question,
many elements, especially those close to uranium in the chemists' rogues'
gallery, were bombarded with neutrons. It was soon observed that thorium,
atomic number 90, would also break into fragments on neutron bombardment.
As in the case of uranium, the fission energy was also of the order of
200 M.e.v. Here again the fission fragments were medium-weight elements.
In 1939, Von Grosse, Booth, and Dunning, working at Columbia University,
found that the rare element protactinium (number 91) would also split when
bombarded with neutrons. No clear-cut evidence of fission could be found
in any other elements tested.

An extremely important difference was noted in the speeds of the neutrons
required to cause fission in these three elements. Whereas only fast neutrons
could cause fission in thorium and protactinium, slow neutrons were found
effective with uranium. This fact was later to become an important link in
the chain of development of the peacetime use of atomic energy.

When talking about atomic particles we have peculiar standards of speed.
A slow neutron might be moving at the speed of about 1 mile per second
(several thousand miles per hour), while a fast neutron might dart about at
many thousand miles per second!
