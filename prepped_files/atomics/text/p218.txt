It was a half century after the invention of Watt's practical steam engine
before it was applied with any degree of success to the steamship. It took
somewhat longer to apply it to the steam locomotive. Now the means were
established for carrying the coal, the energy source of these engines, into all
parts of the world so that engines could be set up anywhere. The gradual
growth over the globe of these threads of transportation, the steamship and
the railroad, rapidly extended the scope of power-driven machinery and knit
together our globe into a complex industrial fabric.

The golden age of discovery . . .

Side by side with the enormous expansion of the use of mechanical power
came the development of science, spurred on by the needs of rapidly growing
industry for new engines, new sources of power, and new materials and in
turn spurring industry on with an ever-increasing volume of new facts and
laws that could be applied to practical purposes.

During the nineteenth century an enormous parallel development of science
and industry took place. This was the century in which the major energy forms
of heat, mechanical energy (motion), radiant energy (light, infrared, ultraviolet,
x-ray, radio), electricity (and magnetism), chemical and other forms
of energy were studied. It was soon discovered that any of these forms of energy
could be converted into any other.

Thus it was found that chemical changes could produce electric currents.
The practical outcome of this fact was the electric battery. The reverse was
also found to be true, and electric currents were used to produce chemical
changes. This was applied in the extremely useful process of electrolysis
and electroplating.
