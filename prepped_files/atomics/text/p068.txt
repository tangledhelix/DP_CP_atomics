In 1911, as a result of these experiments, Rutherford proposed the solar-system
model of the atom, soon to be extended by the Danish Nobel prize
winner, Niels Bohr.

According to this view, an atom consists of an extremely minute core, in
which practically all the mass is concentrated. This core is called the nucleus
and carries a positive charge. Surrounding this nucleus and occupying a
space that is thousands of times greater, are sufficient electrons to balance
the charge on the nucleus exactly. Thus, if the nuclear charge is 10 positive
(+) units, then the atom will have 10 electrons around the nucleus with a
total of 10 negative (--) units of electric charge. In analogy to our own solar
system, these electrons are called planetary electrons. The major portion of the
atoms simply consists of empty space.

A fly in a cathedral . .

Let us consider the simplest atom, that of hydrogen. The nucleus consists of
a single proton, or positive unit of electricity. Since the charge on the nucleus
is 1, there is only one planetary electron.
The space swept out by the motion
of this one electron in its orbit
about the nucleus defines the size of
the atom. The nucleus occupies a
volume so small that it has been likened
to a "fly inside a cathedral,"
with the cathedral representing the
size of the atom! All matter, then, is
primarily empty space. The tiny nuclei
of atoms contain over 99.9 per
cent of the mass.

If it were possible to squeeze matter
together until the nuclei touched one another, then the entire earth could be
compressed to the size of a football. On some distant "white dwarf" stars the
material of which the star is composed is thought to be so dense that a handful
weighs many tons. But even here most of the star is still empty space!
