Finding a good preservative for blood . . .

During the war radioactive iron proved to be of great value in helping to
find a good preservative for whole blood, badly needed for battle casualties,
the blood is well-preserved, then a large fraction of the red cells in the
donor's blood will continue to circulate actively in the patient's blood for
several days, even if the blood was drawn several weeks earlier. How could
this be tested in an efficient manner? How was it possible to distinguish red
cells in the donor's blood from those normally in the blood of the patient
The problem was solved with tagged atoms.

A salt containing radioactive iron was added to the food of the prospective
blood donor. After a certain time, this radioactive iron became part of the
red cells circulating in his blood stream. Some of his blood containing these
tagged iron atoms was then withdrawn. One of several possible preservatives
was then added. After a specified time the blood was transfused into the
patient. From time to time small samples of the recipient's blood were tested
for the presence of the radioactive iron. The survival value of the donor's
blood was measured by the amount of radioactivity observed. A high radioactivity
indicated a high rate of survival of donated blood and the use of a
good preservative. This detective work with tagged atoms of iron thus led
to the discovery of an effective preservative for blood.

We are only just beginning to understand the role of vitamins inside the
body--how they are stored and how fast they are excreted. The molecule
of vitamin B, contains sulfur. In a series of studies vitamin B, was made
using radioactive sulfur. It was found that about 10 per cent of this vitamin
in the human body is destroyed every 24 hours. By accumulating information
of this kind it will eventually be possible to close wide gaps in our knowledge
of the functioning of vitamins and other foods in our bodies.
