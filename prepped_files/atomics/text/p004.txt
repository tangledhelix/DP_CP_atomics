Historians of the future, called upon to classify our existence upon the earth
into "ages" of time, may very well mark the year 1942, when the first atomic-energy
machine was operated, as the beginning of adulthood for mankind
his entrance into the Atomic Age. Man's gropings through the past 20,000
years, his constant struggles against natural forces, and his gradual accumulation
of knowledge have brought him to the point where he can control the very
sources of energy which heretofore have been reserved to nature.

The story starts with fire . . .

Imagine the trials and tribulations of the first man who sought to control
the energy of fire. The beautiful, dancing flame was capable of destroying all
life in its path, tearing apart the material of which living things are made and
turning them into ashes and smoke. With this energy under control, man
could heat his home during the long, cold winter months and keep alive
until the coming of spring. With it he could obtain light to pierce the gloom
of the night and could protect himself from prowling animals.

Through the interminable generations of primitive development, man
learned gradually how to use the energy of fire. He discovered how to cook
his food with it. He found ways of heating certain rocks and fashioning the
metals that flowed out of them into tools and utensils. He learned how to
make new materials with the heat of the flame--glass and brick to build his
cities.

In the past two centuries, under the impetus of the scientific method, man's
ability to use and control the energy of fire has grown by leaps and bounds.
He learned how to use it to operate the steam engine, thus opening wide the
gates of our modern industrial civilization. He applied this new engine to the
steamship and the railroad, to the creation of electricity and the giant communication
networks that now span the globe. He discovered new ways of
using oil to operate more efficient and better designed gasoline, diesel, and j ct-propulsion
engines, which he has applied to new forms of transportation on
the ground and in the air, transforming our huge globe into a more compact
earth.

We go underground to fly . . .

To obtain the energy of the fuels needed to spark this new civilization, man
had to burrow deep into the earth. So far as we know, the oil that we obtain
from the earth--so essential to our industrial structure--will not last more
than a few centuries at the present rate of consumption. The known supply
within the borders of the United States cannot last more than a few decades.
