electron but carrying a single positive charge, in other words, a positive electron,

He hesitated to publish his conclusion until he had found other clear-cut
cases that could be explained only by the existence of a positive electron. He
found more evidence within a few weeks and announced his conclusions to
the world in September, 1932. During the next few months, many other clear-cut
photographs confirmed this conclusion. Soon additional confirmation
appeared from pioneering experiments in other lands by Chadwick, Blackett,
and Occhialini working in England, by M. and Mme Joliot working in France
and by Meitner and Philipp in Germany. In January, 1934, the Joliots bombarded
magnesium, boron, and aluminum targets with alpha particles from
polonium and found that these target atoms became radioactive and emitted
positrons. In other words, the positive electron was rapidly becoming a household
word in the home of nuclear physics. Lauritsen in the same year found
that boron and carbon emit these same particles after being bombarded with
high-energy deuterons. Anderson was awarded a Nobel prize for his pioneering
work in establishing the existence of this new particle.

The positron does not seem to play the same important role in the structure
of the atom as do the neutron and the proton. Yet it cannot be said that it is
rare in nature. It appears that there are about as many positrons as electrons
in cosmic radiations. Furthermore, a number of radioactive atoms created in
the laboratory eject positrons as they undergo transmutation.
