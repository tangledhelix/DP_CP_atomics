of extraction. Cheap heat for distillation and power might make it

these materials are bromine and magnesium (at present extracted from sea

some small amounts of gold, silver, and other metals.

By far the greatest immediate assistance in the way of new chemicals and
compounds will come from the variety of new isotopes which can be manufactured
in the pile. A vast assortment of new materials both for experimentation
and use is now a reality. The story of these uses is described in the next
two chapters.

Atomic energy in packages of all sizes . . .

From what has been related up to this point, it would seem that atomic
energy can only come in "large packages" such as uranium piles and atomic
bombs. It is quite true that chain-reacting machines used to generate power
must be greater than the critical size. But there is a way of obtaining and
using atomic energy in small packages! This time we can use the energy
liberated when unstable radioactive atoms break up to form new atoms, provided
we can make enough unstable atoms. And this can now be done.

An early hint concerning this source of energy came from radium. The
energy liberated from a small sample of radium is sufficient to keep it appreciably
warmer than the surrounding air. A large enough quantity of
radium could boil water and operate steam engines in very much the same way
as a pile. But, unlike the pile, the radium does not have to possess a minimum
critical size before it will operate. No chain reaction is needed; the disintegration
of atoms takes place at a regular rate, unalterable by ordinary means.
Thus, a small quantity of radium will produce heat. Furthermore, the radiations
would be far easier to shield than in the case of the pile. Of course we
know that there is not enough naturally radioactive material easily available
ever to make this more than a pipe dream. But the coming of the uranium
pile has revolutionized this picture.

Radioactive materials similar to radium in these respects could be manufactured
in relatively large amounts inside an operating uranium-graphite
pile. Such materials could then be used as packages of atomic energy in any
size desired. Such a source of atomic energy has been dubbed an atomic
storage battery by G. Gamow of George Washington University.
