This normal procedure in creating new chemical plants usually follows a
definite pattern. A discovery is made in the laboratory or a process is first
tried on a test-tube scale. If the idea appears interesting from the commercial
standpoint, a small pilot plant is built. The latter may simply be a large unit
made of glass and located in the laboratory. It is easy and inexpensive to
study the effect of different conditions on this pilot-plant scale. A research
director hopes that most of the pitfalls in the process will be discovered at this
stage. But, even though the pilot-plant experiments arc a success, a cautious
organization will not proceed at once to the construction of a large-scale
plant.

A semi-works will now be erected, with many times the capacity of the pilot
plant but yet considerably smaller than the final large-scale factory. The
process will now be studied intensively on this scale and all variables will be
checked. Improvements noted here may mean savings of millions of dollars
in the ultimate manufacturing process. Long-term runs are carefully studied
on this semi-works level. If the new process has passed these successively
larger tests, only then does it become feasible to proceed with the construction
of the final plant. For this reason it has become a truism in the chemical industry
that there may be a lag of anywhere from 3 to 5 years between test-tube
research and the final manufacturing process.
