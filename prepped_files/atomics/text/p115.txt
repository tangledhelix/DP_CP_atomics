A match flame brought near the electroscope will do the same thing, because
the gases in a flame are partially ionized by the rapid motion of the
molecules as they rearrange themselves in the process of burning. X-rays
will also discharge an electroscope by ionizing the air.

IONS

Thus the coming together of the
leaves of a charged electroscope can
be used to measure the extent of
ionization of the air and can indirectly
be used to detect the presence
of radioactive materials and other
ionizing agents. In fact, the electroscope
is used in this manner to locate
radium that has been lost accidentally.
Occasionally newspaper
accounts describe the search
electroscope for radium thrown
out by mistake into incinerators or
garbage pails. A charged electroscope carried about near the refuse reveals the
presence of the radium by the ionization of the air around it and the consequent
disappearance of the charge on the leaves.,

Slow leakage of the charge on an electroscope . . .

The interesting observation was made that the leaves on a charged electroscope
gradually come together even when known sources of radioactive
material or x-rays have been removed and all known leakage has been accounted
for. Apparently there was some leakage due to ions existing in the air.
What caused this ionization? Why should it be present in the air at all times?

When radioactive substances were discovered, it was quite natural for
scientists to assume that this ionization was caused by radiations from radium
and other naturally radioactive substances in the surface of the earth. It seemed
most simple to assume that the surface of the earth or the air immediately
above it contained the origin of these ionizing radiations. Experiments designed
to test this idea were begun as early as 1903. In one experiment, an
electroscope was surrounded by a brick container. In another experiment, this
instrument was submerged in a large tank of water. In both cases, the ionization
was more feeble as a result of the shielding action of the surrounding
material.
