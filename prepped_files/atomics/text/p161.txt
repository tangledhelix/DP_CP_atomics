A squash court makes history . . .

In the fall of 1942, the first operating atomic-energy machine was built
on a timber framework that rested on the floor of a squash court underneath
the west stands of Stagg Field, at the University of Chicago. The
pile had the general shape of a doorknob, flattened at the top. Lumps of
uranium and uranium oxide were spaced inside the graphite. In order to
recapture the dramatic nature of this experiment, let us recall that there
is a critical size to a pile. Once enough layers of uranium and graphite have
been laid on top of each other, the pile is theoretically able to start itself.
For there are always enough neutrons in the air as a result of cosmic-ray
bombardment from the upper atmosphere to start the chain reaction going.
Fortunately, it is easy enough to prevent a pile of the critical size or greater
from exploding. It was well known that certain materials, like strips of
cadmium metal, are good neutron robbers and have the ability of absorbing
tremendous numbers of neutrons, thus robbing the chain and stopping
it. These strips, used as controls, were placed
in various slots inside the pile under construction.

Over 12,000 pounds of uranium metal were
used in this construction. The layers were
built up gradually. From time to time, the
cadmium control strips were cautiously pulled
out and measurements were made to see how
far away the pile was from the critical condition.
As long as the pile was smaller than the
critical size, then neutrons shot into the pile
would gradually die out, and the rate at which
they died out was a measure of how far the
pile was from the critical size. Finally, the
measurements showed that the pile was practically
at the critical size. Neutron counters,
safety rods, and automatic control rods were
set in place.

The stage was all set for the operation of this first atomic-energy machine!
