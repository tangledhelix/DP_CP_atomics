
The rogues' gallery of the chemist included 92 case histories. We have
mentioned the leading suspects. Although barium and lanthanum appeared
quite guilty, it seemed as if they were too far from the "scene of the crime."
As a result suspicion was focused on their "older brothers," radium and actinium,
whose case histories arc filed very close to that for uranium.

At this point, Hahn and Strassman, in an effort to establish without any
doubt that radium was formed, inaugurated a very careful series of chemical
tests. They planned to eliminate all other elements besides radium. Then
they could prove that one of the active products resembled radium in every
respect, their case would be clearly established. After much work, they succeeded
in eliminating all elements except barium (atomic number 56) and
radium (atomic number 88). All they had to do now was to show that the
active products behaved as "radium." However, to their amazement, they
found that the product resembled barium!

The conclusion was unmistakable now. Contrary to all previous guesses,
uranium atoms, on neutron bombardment, split into fragments about the
middle of the periodic table. The products were well-known elements. Yet
for 5 years chemists and physicists had been looking for these products in
the confusing region of elements beyond uranium.

This breakdown of uranium into two light fragments was called uranium
fission. This fission process was thus established by the chemical experiments of
Hahn and Strassman in 1939 after one of the most exciting scientific hunts
in history.

200 M.e.v from every fission . . .

Although the search for the identity of the products resulting from the
neutron bombardment of uranium had required 5 years of research in various
parts of the world, it required only a few hours for the physicist to realize
the exciting significance of the fact that uranium had been split into two
lighter atoms. This was no ordinary atom-chipping reaction, of which hundreds
were already known.

A study of a table of atomic weights readily shows that the sum of the
masses of two light nuclei that might result from fission is less (in almost every
case) than that of the parent uranium atom. According to Einstein's simple
relationship between mass and energy, this loss in mass would show up as energy
associated with the breakdown of the uranium atom. This energy would
appear in the form of tremendous speeds with which the fragments shot
away from the scene of fission. Part of the energy would be in the form of
high-speed electrons and high-energy gamma rays. Any competent physicist
could easily estimate the amount of energy produced.
