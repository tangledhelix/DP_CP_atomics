Energy is life the "old man of the sea" . . .

Indeed, energy makes itself apparent to us in a great variety of forms. We
have come to accept two of these in an almost intuitive manner. The first is
the energy that an object has simply because it is moving--a swinging bat, a
moving ball, an arrow in flight. This we call kinetic energy. Now consider a
ball on the very top of a hill, an arrow in a bow that has been bent but not
released, a jack-in-the-box before the lid is raised. Although there is no motion
in any of these examples, yet we know that the objects could be set in motion
very easily--by allowing the ball to fall, releasing the arrow, and raising the
jack-in-the-box lid. This stored-up energy is called potential energy.

What happens when the arrow is released? The potential energy in the
stretched bowstring is converted into kinetic energy of the moving arrow.
Stop a swing at its highest point. This is the position of greatest potential
energy. Now release the swing and the motion becomes most rapid at the
lowest point, for here the potential energy has been transformed into energy
of motion.

Water behind a dam is a good example of potential (stored-up) energy,
which it possesses because of its higher position. At the top of the dam it is
practically motionless. Once it enters the pipe to the power house, it falls with
great speed, turning the huge turbine wheels and supplying them with kinetic
energy. The motion of the turbine can now produce electric energy to be distributed
to distant homes and factories.

All fuels have chemical potential energy, which is stored up until the fuel
is burned. Thereupon the heat of the flame can be transformed into motion
in a steam, diesel, or gasoline engine.

What is heat? . . .

The energy of moving objects was one of the first forms to be studied. In
the latter part of the seventeenth century, Sir Isaac Newton achieved brilliant
success in these studies. Aided by the rules of motion which he discovered,
scientists could explain the movements of the distant planets. Today we apply
these rules to the flight of airplanes, the building of bridges, and the manifold
physical problems of our giant industrial civilization.
