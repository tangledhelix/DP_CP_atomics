Energy, on the other hand is a far more elusive idea. It was not until a century
or so ago that the concept of energy as existing in numerous forms,
convertible one into the other, was developed. After numerous experiments
and calculations during the nineteenth century, the fundamental principle of
conservation of energy was brought forth, which postulated that energy cannot
be created or destroyed but can only be changed into other forms.

Einstein showed that energy and matter are equivalent . . .

Thus for many years the two principles reigned supreme side by side--the
conservation of mass and the conservation of energy--all through the nineteenth
century and up to 1905 when
Einstein tied the knot which bound
both these principles into one. According
to his theory, matter and energy
are related to each other in an
intimate manner, with a definite
quantity of matter equivalent to a
definite quantity of energy. Further,
he stated that one can be converted
into the other.

Thus, matter can be destroyed and
converted into energy. Energy can
be destroyed and matter created.
There is conservation, not of energy
or matter separately, but of matter plus energy taken together.

But Einstein did more than simply state that matter and energy can be
converted into each other. He presented a simple formula which enables the
reader to calculate the amount of energy that is liberated for a given loss in
mass. This relation states
