its power. Besides, how could we carry the coal to other places where the

did make a tremendous advance when he fitted a sliding piston into
a cylinder and utilized the push of the steam to make the piston move. This
has remained the basic pattern of most of our modern heat engines.

Steam and gasoline . . .

It took more than half a century for Newcomen's engine to develop into a
relatively efficient prime mover that could be adapted to other uses such
as trains and boats. Credit for the basic improvements in the steam engine
goes to James Watt, who brought forth the modern steam engine in 1763.
This engine has remained relatively unchanged in basic design until today.

PISTON

These early heat engines, as
well as our most modern turbines
and gasoline engines, all utilize
the same basic principles. The
problem in converting heat energy
into motion is to organize
the random motion of molecules
(speeded up by the heat produced
by the combustion of a fuel) so
that they all push one way. At high
temperatures the molecules contained
in a chamber will bombard
the walls in all directions and exert
a considerable pressure. Some
idea of this force may be obtained from the fact that a given amount of water
when boiled and converted into steam at ordinary pressures will produce a
volume of steam about 2,000 times as great as the original volume of the water.
However, if the steam cannot escape, its tendency to expand causes an enormous
pressure on the walls of the container. The completely random motion
of the molecules exerting this pressure can then be utilized by making it
possible for one part of the container to move. Thus the piston in a steam engine
can move while the walls cannot. Hence the bombardment of the molecules
pushes the piston.
