element) would have unique chemical properties. As a result, it should be
possible to separate it by straightforward chemical means.

You will observe that the process of production of plutonium that we have
described takes place during the operation of the uranium pile. In the
previous chapter we were concerned solely with obtaining a sufficient
amount of uranium in the pile to reach the critical size and keep the chain
reaction going. We were interested only in the energy produced by the
uranium pile. Now, we sec that the bombardment of U238 by neutrons
has a useful by-product. The capture of a neutron by U238 leads to the formation
of a plutonium atom.

In fact, the important properties of plutonium quickly turned the tables and
stimulated us to build uranium piles solely to get the plutonium, with the
large amounts of energy becoming a wasteful and even harmful by-product.

Bomb in 70,000 years using the first pile . . .

Some arithmetic will tell us very quickly that the first chain-reacting pile
in history, which was described in the previous chapter, was woefully inadequate
for the purpose of producing the needed plutonium. It was estimated
that a bomb might require an amount of plutonium ranging from 2 to 200
pounds. Smyth reveals in his report that a pile producing about 2 pounds
of plutonium per day would be liberating energy at the rate of 500,000 to
1,500,000 kilowatts (a kilowatt is 1,000 watts--about 1-1/3 horsepower). But
the first chain-reacting pile set up at the University of Chicago was operated
at a maximum of about 200 watts (or 75 kilowatt). At this rate, it would
take at least 70,000 years to create the plutonium needed for one bomb!
It thus became a matter of urgent necessity to design and build a pile that
would furnish the needed plutonium in a reasonably rapid time--not centuries!
